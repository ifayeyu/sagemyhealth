    🔬 Tests to ask your doctor for:

    What is anemia?

    Anemia occurs when your blood doesn't have enough healthy red blood cells — or when those cells don't contain enough haemoglobin — to carry adequate oxygen to your tissues. Every cell in your body needs oxygen to function, so when delivery falls short, fatigue sets in fast.

    There are over 400 types of anemia, but most fall into three buckets: blood loss (heavy periods, GI bleeding), reduced production (iron, B12, or folate deficiency), or increased destruction (haemolytic conditions). Iron deficiency is the most common cause by far — and one of the most fixable.

    Iron-deficiency
    ~50% of cases
    Fatigue, pica, pale skin, spoon nails, restless legs. Ask: ferritin, serum iron, TIBC.
    B12 / Folate deficiency
    ~5–10%
    Tongue soreness, nerve tingling, mood changes. Ask: serum B12, RBC folate.
    Other causes
    ~40–45%
    Chronic disease, kidney disease, haemolysis, bone marrow issues. Full CBC required.

    Why anemia disproportionately affects women

    Women of reproductive age lose iron-rich blood every month through menstruation. Heavy periods can deplete iron faster than diet replenishes it. Pregnancy dramatically increases iron demand — and many women enter pregnancy already iron-depleted without knowing it.

    Global statistics on women & anemia
    37%pregnant women affected worldwide
    30%non-pregnant women of reproductive age

    Many GPs order only a CBC (complete blood count) and may miss iron deficiency if haemoglobin is still normal. Always ask specifically for ferritin — it measures your iron stores and can be low even when haemoglobin looks fine.

    Understanding Your Anaemia Risk Assessment

    Anaemia — defined as haemoglobin below 12 g/dL in women and below 13 g/dL in men — is not a diagnosis in itself but a sign with over 400 possible underlying causes. The most common are iron deficiency (accounting for roughly 50% of all anaemia worldwide), vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, chronic disease, haemolysis, and bone marrow dysfunction. Each type has a distinct mechanism, a distinct treatment, and a distinct prognosis. What they share is the physiological consequence of reduced oxygen-carrying capacity: fatigue, exercise intolerance, cognitive impairment, pallor, and shortness of breath on exertion. This screener assesses your symptom burden and risk profile to estimate how likely anaemia is to be contributing to your experience.

    What Your Score Means

    Low Suspicion
    Low score range
    Few anaemia risk factors or symptoms are present. If you are experiencing fatigue, other causes — sleep, stress, thyroid, or mood disorders — are more likely to be primary.
    Moderate Suspicion
    Mid score range
    Some risk factors or symptoms are present. A complete blood count (CBC) with differential and ferritin is a simple, low-cost way to either confirm or rule out anaemia as a contributor.
    High Suspicion
    High score range
    Multiple risk factors and symptoms are present. A medical evaluation is recommended. Not all anaemias are iron-related — CBC with differential, B12, folate, ferritin, reticulocyte count, and potentially peripheral blood smear may all be indicated.

    What to Do Next

    1. Request a CBC (complete blood count) with differential as the first step. This basic panel reveals haemoglobin, haematocrit, red blood cell size (MCV), and other indices that allow your doctor to narrow the type of anaemia before treatment. Treating iron-deficiency anaemia with B12 — or vice versa — is ineffective and can cause harm.
    2. Know that the type of anaemia determines the treatment. Iron-deficiency anaemia is treated with iron supplementation. B12-deficiency anaemia (often from poor absorption rather than poor intake) is treated with B12 supplementation or injections. Anaemia of chronic disease requires treating the underlying condition. Never self-treat with supplements before knowing your type.
    3. Investigate the cause of iron-deficiency anaemia, not just the deficiency itself. In men and post-menopausal women, iron-deficiency anaemia without a clear dietary explanation requires investigation for gastrointestinal blood loss, including colon cancer screening if age-appropriate.
    4. Monitor energy, exercise tolerance, and concentration as functional markers of treatment response. Haemoglobin typically takes 4–8 weeks to rise meaningfully with iron supplementation; ferritin takes 3–6 months to fully restore. Functional improvement often precedes laboratory normalisation.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What is the difference between anaemia and iron deficiency?

    Iron deficiency is a continuum: it begins with depleted ferritin (iron stores), progresses to reduced serum iron and transferrin saturation, and only in the final stage does haemoglobin fall — which is when anaemia develops. Iron deficiency without anaemia is common and still causes significant fatigue and hair shedding.

    Can anaemia cause anxiety or depression?

    Yes. Reduced oxygen delivery to the brain impairs neurotransmitter synthesis and energy metabolism. Chronic anaemia is associated with worsened mood, increased anxiety, and cognitive slowing. These symptoms often improve significantly with effective anaemia treatment.

    Is anaemia dangerous during pregnancy?

    Significant anaemia during pregnancy is associated with increased risks of preterm birth, low birth weight, and postpartum haemorrhage. Pregnancy dramatically increases iron requirements (to approximately 27 mg/day compared to 18 mg/day for non-pregnant women), and routine supplementation with prenatal vitamins is standard of care. Haemoglobin and ferritin are typically monitored at booking and throughout pregnancy.

    How quickly does anaemia improve with treatment?

    With appropriate treatment, symptoms often begin improving within 2–4 weeks. Haemoglobin typically rises meaningfully within 4–8 weeks. Iron stores (ferritin) take 3–6 months to fully replenish. B12-deficiency anaemia may show faster haematological response but neurological recovery can take months to years depending on duration of deficiency.

    What foods are highest in absorbable iron?

    Haem iron (most efficiently absorbed): beef, lamb, liver, oysters, dark chicken meat. Non-haem iron (less efficiently absorbed but bioavailability improved by vitamin C): lentils, tofu, fortified cereals, spinach, pumpkin seeds. Combining a vitamin C source (bell pepper, orange, broccoli) with non-haem iron sources can double or triple absorption.
