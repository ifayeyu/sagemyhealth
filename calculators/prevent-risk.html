    What is the PREVENT model?

    The Predicting Risk of cardiovascular disease EVENTs (PREVENT) equations were published by the American Heart Association in 2024 (Khan SS et al., Circulation). They replace the 2013 Pooled Cohort Equations with key improvements: they are completely race-free, validated in adults aged 30–79, and add kidney function (eGFR) and BMI as predictors.

    PREVENT estimates three outcomes: Total CVD (heart attacks + strokes + heart failure), ASCVD (heart attacks + strokes), and Heart Failure — each at 10-year and 30-year horizons.

    Understanding your ASCVD risk tier

    Low (<3%) — Very low risk. Lifestyle focus only; statins not recommended for primary prevention.

    Borderline (3–4.9%) — Slightly elevated. Risk-enhancing factors (family history, high CRP, CAC score) may tip toward a statin discussion.

    Intermediate (5–9.9%) — Moderate risk where a statin discussion is recommended under 2026 ACC/AHA guidelines.

    High (≥10%) — High risk where statin therapy is typically recommended alongside lifestyle changes and close monitoring.

    Understanding the AHA PREVENT Equations

    The Predicting Risk of cardiovascular disease EVENTs (PREVENT) equations, published in Circulation in 2024, represent the most significant update to cardiovascular risk prediction in over a decade. Developed by Khan SS and colleagues from pooled data of 6.6 million adults across 25 US health systems, PREVENT addresses key limitations of its predecessor — the 2013 Pooled Cohort Equations — by removing race as a biological variable, expanding the age range to 30–79, incorporating kidney function (eGFR) and body mass index as independent predictors, and providing both 10-year and 30-year risk estimates across three cardiovascular outcomes: total CVD, ASCVD, and heart failure.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    How is PREVENT different from the old ASCVD Pooled Cohort Equations?

    The Pooled Cohort Equations (PCE, 2013) required race as a mandatory input and were validated only in White and Black Americans aged 40–79. They also predicted only one outcome (ASCVD) over one time horizon (10 years). PREVENT eliminates race entirely, extends the age range to 30–79, adds eGFR and BMI, provides both 10-year and 30-year estimates, and is validated in a far more diverse and contemporary population. Studies show PREVENT is better calibrated across all demographic groups, often producing meaningfully different risk estimates from the old equations.

    What does my PREVENT score mean for statin decisions?

    According to the 2026 ACC/AHA Dyslipidemia guidelines, a 10-year ASCVD risk below 3% is considered low — statins are not recommended for primary prevention. A risk of 3–5% is borderline — risk-enhancing factors (family history, elevated CRP, CAC score) may prompt a discussion. A risk of 5–10% is intermediate — a statin conversation with your doctor is actively recommended. At 10% or above, statin therapy is typically recommended alongside lifestyle changes.

    What is eGFR and why does it matter for heart risk?

    eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate) measures how well your kidneys filter waste from your blood. It appears on routine blood test panels and is automatically calculated from creatinine levels. Reduced kidney function is a strong, independent predictor of cardiovascular events and heart failure — it causes arterial stiffness, hypertension, and chronic inflammation, all of which accelerate cardiovascular disease. Values below 60 indicate chronic kidney disease and substantially increase risk. PREVENT is the first major CVD risk equation to formally incorporate eGFR as a predictor.

    Why do the 30-year estimates seem high?

    30-year risk estimates reflect the cumulative probability of a cardiovascular event over three decades — even people with good current health will show substantial 30-year risk simply because a 30-year window is very long. These estimates are most clinically useful for younger adults (under 50) where 10-year risk appears deceptively low despite unfavorable risk factors that will compound over time. The 30-year estimate can motivate early intervention. Note that PREVENT 30-year estimates are considered less precise for adults over 59.

    Does a high heart failure risk need different treatment than ASCVD risk?

    Yes — the treatment approaches differ meaningfully. ASCVD (heart attacks and strokes from atherosclerosis) is primarily managed with statins and blood pressure control. Heart failure risk is more heavily driven by blood pressure, diabetes, kidney function, and obesity. Aggressive blood pressure control, weight management, and diabetes treatment have disproportionately large effects on heart failure risk compared with ASCVD risk. The PREVENT equations show both risks separately precisely because someone might have high HF risk but modest ASCVD risk — and their treatment priorities should differ accordingly.
