A few things worth knowing:

    What does blood glucose measure?

    Blood glucose is the amount of sugar (glucose) circulating in your bloodstream at any given moment. Your body regulates this tightly — too low and your cells don't have enough energy, too high over time and it can damage blood vessels and organs. Where your reading falls depends heavily on when you tested, what you ate, and whether you have diabetes.

    What Your Fasting Blood Glucose Really Means

    Blood glucose is the amount of sugar circulating in your bloodstream at any given moment. After an overnight fast of at least 8 hours — the standard condition for this measurement — your result reflects how efficiently your pancreas and liver are regulating baseline glucose metabolism. The American Diabetes Association's thresholds, which this calculator uses, are derived from large epidemiological studies that identified the glucose levels at which long-term complications — retinopathy, neuropathy, kidney disease, and cardiovascular damage — begin to accumulate. The prediabetes range is not a buffer zone: it carries genuine biological risk and a meaningful window for intervention.

    What Your Score Means

    Normal
    <100 mg/dL (<5.6 mmol/L)
    Healthy glucose metabolism. The pancreas is producing insulin effectively and cells are responding appropriately.
    Prediabetes
    100–125 mg/dL (5.6–6.9 mmol/L)
    Impaired fasting glucose. Without intervention, approximately 15–30% of people in this range develop type 2 diabetes within 5 years — but research shows this is largely reversible.
    Diabetes Range
    ≥126 mg/dL (≥7.0 mmol/L)
    This result warrants prompt medical follow-up. Two separate fasting readings above 126, or a single result above 200 with symptoms, meets ADA diagnostic criteria for diabetes.

    What to Do Next

    1. Don't rely on a single fasting reading. The ADA requires two abnormal values on separate days to diagnose diabetes. Glucose can be transiently elevated after illness, significant stress, or steroid use.
    2. If you're in the prediabetes range, act now — the window is open. The landmark Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP) trial showed that lifestyle intervention — modest weight loss plus 150 minutes of weekly exercise — reduced progression to diabetes by 58%.
    3. Request an HbA1c alongside your fasting glucose. Haemoglobin A1c reflects your average blood glucose over the previous 2–3 months and is less susceptible to day-to-day variation. Together, both tests give a far more complete picture.
    4. Examine what elevates glucose beyond food. Chronic poor sleep, emotional stress, low physical activity, and certain medications (corticosteroids, some antipsychotics, diuretics) all raise fasting glucose independently of diet.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Do I need to fast for exactly 8 hours?

    Yes — the 8-hour minimum is clinically important. Eating or drinking anything caloric within that window raises your glucose and invalidates the fasting result. Plain water is fine; coffee or tea should be avoided.

    What is the difference between fasting glucose and HbA1c?

    Fasting glucose is a real-time measurement. HbA1c reflects the proportion of red blood cells that have been glycated over the past 60–90 days, giving a longer-term average. Each can be normal or abnormal independently of the other, which is why both are useful.

    Can I have diabetes with a normal fasting glucose?

    Yes, in some cases. Post-meal glucose spikes detected with a 2-hour glucose tolerance test or HbA1c can be elevated even when fasting glucose is normal. This is called isolated post-challenge hyperglycaemia and carries cardiovascular risk.

    How do stress and poor sleep affect blood glucose?

    Both activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, raising cortisol and adrenaline — hormones that drive glucose upward through hepatic glucose production and insulin resistance. Consistently sleeping under 6 hours is associated with fasting glucose levels 6–10% higher than those sleeping 7–9 hours.

    What foods lower fasting blood glucose most effectively?

    No single food is a treatment. The most evidence-backed approaches are higher fibre intake, reduced refined carbohydrates, protein at each meal to blunt post-meal spikes, and fermented foods before meals, which have shown modest glucose-lowering effects in small trials.
