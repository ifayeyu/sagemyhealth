About this calculator

Calories burned during exercise is calculated using MET values — Metabolic Equivalents of Task. One MET equals energy burned at rest (~1 kcal/kg/hour). Walking at a moderate pace is about 3.5 MET; running at 6 mph is around 10 MET. The formula is: Calories = MET × weight (kg) × duration (hours). MET values come from the Compendium of Physical Activities (Ainsworth et al.), the gold-standard reference used in exercise science and public health research.

Understanding Your Calories Burned Estimate

The calories-burned estimate you see here is derived from MET values — Metabolic Equivalent of Task units, a standardised system developed by researchers at the American College of Sports Medicine that quantifies the energy cost of physical activities relative to resting metabolism. One MET is defined as the energy expenditure at complete rest; walking briskly carries a MET of approximately 3.5; running at 8 minutes per mile carries a MET of roughly 11.5. Multiplying MET by body weight and duration gives an estimate of total caloric expenditure. While no formula is perfectly precise — individual metabolic variation, fitness level, and body composition all affect accuracy — MET-based estimates are the most widely used and validated method available without laboratory equipment.

What Your Score Means

Light Activity
<3 METs / <200 kcal/hour
Walking slowly, gentle yoga, casual cycling. Valuable for recovery, joint health, and baseline caloric expenditure. Does not typically elevate heart rate to aerobic training zones.
Moderate Activity
3–6 METs / 200–400 kcal/hour
Brisk walking, recreational swimming, dancing, casual cycling. This range meets WHO physical activity guidelines and carries established cardiovascular and metabolic benefits.
Vigorous Activity
6–9 METs / 400–700 kcal/hour
Running, HIIT, vigorous swimming, competitive sports. Produces significant cardiovascular adaptation and the highest energy expenditure per unit time.
Very Vigorous
>9 METs / >700 kcal/hour
Elite athletic training loads. High caloric output but also high injury and overtraining risk if recovery is not proportionally managed.

What to Do Next

  1. Use this number as a benchmark, not a precise measurement. MET-based formulas underestimate expenditure in very fit individuals (who are more efficient) and can overestimate in sedentary individuals. Wearable devices combining heart rate data with MET estimates improve accuracy by roughly 20%.
  2. Don't create a large caloric deficit through exercise alone. Exercise-induced hunger often compensates for a portion of calories burned. Research by Lund et al. suggests the body compensates for approximately 50–75% of exercise-induced energy expenditure through increased appetite and reduced non-exercise energy expenditure.
  3. Prioritise consistency over intensity. Public health data consistently show that regular moderate-intensity exercise maintained for years produces far greater health outcomes than sporadic high-intensity training. The best workout is one you will repeat for decades.
  4. Pair your calorie output with your TDEE for context. A single session's expenditure becomes meaningful when you understand your total daily energy expenditure. Use our TDEE calculator to see this number in context of your full 24-hour energy picture.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do heavier people burn more calories doing the same activity?

Yes. Calorie expenditure scales with body weight because moving a heavier body requires proportionally more energy. A 90 kg person burns roughly 50% more calories than a 60 kg person doing the same activity at the same intensity.

Why does my fitness tracker give a different number?

Fitness trackers use proprietary algorithms that combine MET estimates with heart rate data, step counts, and sometimes skin temperature. These are more personalised but also subject to device-specific errors. MET-based formulas and wearable estimates typically differ by 10–30%.

Should I eat back the calories I burn exercising?

This depends on your goals. For fat loss, most evidence suggests not eating back all exercise calories — a modest deficit is the goal. For athletic performance or weight maintenance, fuelling your training appropriately is essential. Your total energy needs (TDEE) account for your typical activity level.

Does muscle mass affect how many calories I burn during exercise?

Modestly. Muscle tissue is metabolically active and increases resting metabolic rate, but the direct effect during exercise is secondary to body weight and intensity. The bigger effect of higher muscle mass is the increase in resting caloric expenditure throughout the other 23 hours of your day.

Is calorie counting useful for weight management?

Counting provides useful self-awareness, especially for people who habitually underestimate their intake. However, calorie labelling carries precision that is often misleading — regulatory guidelines in the US allow a 20% margin of error on food labels. Pattern-level tracking (meals, food quality, hunger cues) is often more sustainable than precise calorie arithmetic.
