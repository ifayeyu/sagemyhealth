What to do next:

    This screener cannot diagnose a deficiency — only blood tests can. The results should guide the conversation with your doctor about which tests to request. A ferritin (stored iron) level is the single most useful first test for hair-loss-related nutritional deficiency.

    Why nutrition is the first place to look

    Hair follicles are among the most metabolically active cells in the body — they're highly sensitive to nutritional deficits. Because hair growth is non-essential, the body deprioritises follicle nutrition during times of scarcity. This is why nutritional hair loss typically begins 2–4 months after a deficiency develops, and continues for months after correction.

    Of all nutritional causes, low ferritin (stored iron) is the most common and most underdiagnosed in women. Many doctors use a serum ferritin cut-off that's too low for hair health — optimal ferritin for hair growth is generally considered to be above 70 ng/mL, yet labs often flag 12–15 ng/mL as "normal."

    Your Hair & Nutritional Deficiency Risk Score

    Hair follicles are among the most metabolically active cells in the human body — and among the first to signal nutritional stress. When the body experiences a caloric deficit, protein restriction, or micronutrient shortage, it prioritises resources toward vital organ function and diverts them away from non-essential tissues like hair. The result is increased shedding, impaired growth, changes in hair texture, and in severe cases, diffuse thinning. This screener evaluates the nutritional risk factors most strongly associated with hair health impairment — including iron, zinc, biotin, vitamin D, protein intake, and omega-3 status — to help you identify the most likely dietary contributors to any hair concerns you're experiencing.

    What to Do Next

    1. Test before supplementing. Iron and vitamin A toxicity are real clinical risks from over-supplementation. Before taking high-dose iron, vitamin D, or zinc, confirm deficiency with blood testing. Ask your doctor for ferritin, 25-OH vitamin D, zinc, and a complete blood count.
    2. Prioritise protein intake, which is often overlooked. Hair is primarily composed of keratin — a protein. Chronic low protein intake (below 0.8 g/kg body weight) increases telogen shedding. Vegetarians and vegans, individuals recovering from illness, and those following restrictive diets are at highest risk.
    3. Focus first on iron and ferritin. Iron deficiency is the most common nutritional deficiency worldwide and the most frequently implicated in hair shedding. Ferritin levels below 30 ng/mL are associated with increased hair loss in women. Many dermatologists aim for ferritin above 70 ng/mL for optimal hair cycling.
    4. Consider an anti-inflammatory dietary pattern. The Mediterranean diet, rich in omega-3 fatty acids, polyphenols, and antioxidants, is associated with better hair density in observational studies. Chronic low-grade inflammation, amplified by highly processed dietary patterns, may affect follicle cycling.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Which nutritional deficiency causes the most hair loss?

    Iron deficiency is the most prevalent and most directly studied cause of nutritional hair shedding. Vitamin D deficiency, zinc deficiency, and inadequate protein intake are also significant contributors. In clinical practice, a full nutritional panel — not just one marker — is usually assessed.

    Can a crash diet cause hair loss?

    Yes — this is one of the most common and underappreciated triggers for telogen effluvium. Significant caloric restriction (especially below 1,200 kcal/day), very low protein intake, or rapid weight loss signal physiological stress that triggers follicles to enter the resting phase prematurely. Shedding typically occurs 2–3 months after the dietary trigger.

    Does omega-3 help with hair growth?

    Omega-3 fatty acids are involved in scalp health and follicle structure. A small randomised controlled trial (Le Floc'h et al., 2015) found improved hair density and reduced hair loss with omega-3 and omega-6 supplementation combined with antioxidants. Evidence is preliminary but biologically plausible.

    How does vitamin D affect hair?

    Vitamin D receptors are expressed in hair follicles and play a role in the hair growth cycle. Deficiency is associated with both telogen effluvium and alopecia areata in several observational studies. Supplementation appears beneficial for people who are genuinely deficient; benefits in non-deficient individuals are unclear.

    Is hair loss after bariatric surgery nutritional?

    Yes, and it is extremely common — affecting up to 70% of patients in the months following bariatric surgery. The causes are multifactorial: dramatic caloric restriction, reduced protein absorption, zinc and iron malabsorption (especially after gastric bypass), and the physiological stress of surgery. In most cases the shedding is temporary but requires active nutritional management to minimise and resolve.
