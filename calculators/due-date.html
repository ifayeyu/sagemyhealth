Things to know:

    About your due date

    Your due date is estimated at 40 weeks from the first day of your last menstrual period (LMP). Most pregnancies last between 38–42 weeks. Due dates calculated from LMP are generally accurate within 3–5 days. Ultrasound dating in the first trimester can narrow this window further. Your healthcare provider will confirm your due date at your first ultrasound.

    Understanding Your Estimated Due Date

    Your estimated due date (EDD) is calculated using Naegele's Rule — a formula established in the early 19th century by German obstetrician Franz Karl Naegele that adds 280 days (40 weeks) to the first day of your last menstrual period. This assumes a 28-day cycle with ovulation on day 14. The EDD is a statistical midpoint, not a deadline: only about 5% of babies are born on their exact due date. The majority are born within two weeks on either side. What the date gives you is a gestational timeline — a framework for tracking fetal development, scheduling screenings, and understanding when labour becomes expected, overdue, or premature.

    What Your Score Means

    Full Term
    39–40 weeks
    The optimal gestational range. Research from ACOG and SMFM shows that outcomes are best for babies born at full term (39–40 weeks), with slightly better lung maturity and feeding outcomes than early term.
    Early Term
    37–38 weeks
    Clinically viable but associated with slightly higher rates of NICU admission and feeding difficulties than full-term births. Elective delivery before 39 weeks without medical indication is generally discouraged.
    Late Term / Post-term
    41–42+ weeks
    Beyond the EDD. Placental function begins to decline and fetal monitoring intensifies. Many providers offer induction after 41 weeks; ACOG recommends induction by 42 weeks in most cases.

    What to Do Next

    1. Ultrasound dating is more accurate than LMP dating. If you had an early ultrasound (ideally before 12 weeks), the gestational age it establishes is more reliable than LMP-based calculation — especially if your cycles are irregular. Your midwife or OB will typically adjust your EDD based on early ultrasound findings.
    2. Build your prenatal care schedule around key gestational milestones. The timing of first trimester screening (10–13 weeks), anatomy scan (18–22 weeks), glucose tolerance test (24–28 weeks), and Group B Strep testing (35–37 weeks) are all tied to your EDD.
    3. Understand that the EDD is a range, not a deadline. The psychological experience of a due date can be distressing when labour doesn't begin on time. Active labour can safely begin from 37–42 weeks. Discuss your provider's induction policies in advance so you understand what to expect.
    4. Start folic acid supplementation immediately if you haven't already. Neural tube closure occurs in weeks 3–4 of gestation — often before pregnancy is confirmed. The CDC recommends 400–800 mcg of folate daily beginning at least 1 month before conception and throughout the first trimester.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    How accurate is my estimated due date?

    The EDD is accurate to within about 2 weeks in either direction for most pregnancies. Early ultrasound (before 12 weeks) can refine this estimate. Dating becomes less precise as pregnancy progresses. Only 5% of babies are born on their exact due date.

    What happens if I go past my due date?

    Going past 40 weeks is common. Most providers increase fetal monitoring (non-stress tests, biophysical profiles) after 40 weeks. ACOG recommends offering induction at 41 weeks and recommends induction by 42 weeks to reduce the small but real risk of stillbirth associated with very post-term pregnancy.

    Can my due date change?

    Your LMP-based estimated due date can be revised based on early ultrasound findings. However, once your EDD has been established (ideally by first-trimester ultrasound), it is not typically changed based on later ultrasounds, as third-trimester fetal size measurements have much wider normal variation.

    What is the difference between gestational age and fetal age?

    Gestational age is counted from the first day of the last menstrual period — which is approximately 2 weeks before conception. Fetal age (also called conceptional age) counts from fertilisation. Your EDD and all prenatal milestones use gestational age, so a baby at 40 weeks gestational age is approximately 38 weeks old post-conception.

    Does being pregnant with twins change my due date?

    Twin pregnancies have a different expected gestational length. Dichorionic-diamniotic (DCDA) twins are often delivered at 38 weeks; monochorionic-diamniotic (MCDA) twins at 36–37 weeks; and monoamniotic twins even earlier. Your OB will establish specific timing goals based on your twin type and any complications.
