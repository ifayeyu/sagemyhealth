This screener cannot diagnose the cause of your hair loss — that requires examination by a doctor, and often blood tests or scalp assessment. These results are designed to guide your conversation with a healthcare provider, not replace it. Multiple causes frequently co-exist.

Why diagnosing the cause matters

Hair loss in women is almost never just "one thing." Yet most women are told "it's stress" or given iron supplements without investigation. The six common causes in women — telogen effluvium, female pattern hair loss, hormonal conditions (PCOS, thyroid, hormonal contraception), nutritional deficiency, alopecia areata, and traction alopecia — each have distinct treatments, timelines, and prognoses.

The pattern of hair loss, its timeline, associated symptoms, and family history collectively paint a clear clinical picture — which is what this screener analyses. A diagnosis that leads to the right treatment can mean the difference between hair that recovers fully in 6 months and hair loss that continues for years untreated.

Understanding Female Pattern Hair Loss — What Your Screener Result Means

Female pattern hair loss (FPHL) — also called androgenetic alopecia in women — is the most common form of hair loss in women, affecting an estimated 40% of women by age 50 and up to 55% by age 70. Unlike male pattern baldness, which typically follows a receding hairline pattern, FPHL in women most commonly presents as diffuse thinning across the crown and parting line, classified by the Ludwig scale (Grades I, II, and III). Despite its prevalence, FPHL is significantly underdiagnosed because many women attribute hair thinning to normal aging or styling. This screener uses the same clinical criteria used by dermatologists to estimate severity and guide next steps.

What Your Score Means

Ludwig Grade I
Mild / Early
Slight widening of the central parting and mild thinning at the crown. Hair loss is noticeable to the patient but may not be apparent to others. Early intervention produces the best outcomes.
Ludwig Grade II
Moderate
Noticeable widening of the part and diffuse thinning at the crown. Hair density reduction is visible and may affect styling choices. Medical evaluation and treatment are strongly recommended.
Ludwig Grade III
Severe
Extensive thinning through the crown with near-complete loss of coverage at the vertex. The frontal hairline is typically preserved. Advanced dermatological treatment, including possible PRP or hair transplant consultation, is appropriate.

What to Do Next

  1. See a dermatologist for a clinical assessment before starting any treatment. FPHL has many overlapping mimics — including telogen effluvium, alopecia areata, scalp psoriasis, and traction alopecia — that require different treatments. A scalp biopsy or dermoscopy can definitively confirm the diagnosis.
  2. Get basic bloodwork to rule out contributing causes. Thyroid dysfunction, iron deficiency anaemia, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), and elevated androgens are all treatable conditions that can cause or significantly worsen hair loss. A panel including TSH, ferritin, total iron, TIBC, testosterone, DHEA-S, and CBC is standard.
  3. Minoxidil (topical) is the only FDA-approved topical treatment for FPHL. A 2% or 5% solution applied to the scalp twice daily has the most evidence. The 5% foam formulation is also approved and often preferred for tolerability. Response takes 3–6 months of consistent use to assess.
  4. Discuss oral options with your dermatologist. Spironolactone (an antiandrogen), oral minoxidil at low doses, and finasteride are used off-label for FPHL with meaningful evidence. These require prescription, monitoring, and for reproductive-age women, specific contraceptive considerations.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is female pattern hair loss permanent?

Partially. The follicle miniaturisation process in FPHL is progressive and does not self-reverse. However, approved treatments (minoxidil, anti-androgens) can halt or significantly slow progression and, in some cases, produce partial regrowth — particularly when started early.

Does diet cause FPHL?

Diet is a contributing factor, not the primary cause. Iron deficiency, zinc deficiency, crash dieting, and very low protein intake can all precipitate or worsen shedding (telogen effluvium). FPHL itself is driven by genetic androgen sensitivity at the follicle level, but nutritional deficiencies can accelerate its progression.

Does biotin supplementation help hair loss?

Biotin deficiency can cause hair loss and is legitimately treated with supplementation. However, biotin deficiency is rare in people with normal diets. The widespread marketing of high-dose biotin for hair growth in non-deficient individuals is not supported by controlled clinical evidence. Importantly, high-dose biotin interferes with several laboratory tests, including thyroid and cardiac troponin assays.

Can stress cause permanent hair loss?

Severe acute stress (physical illness, surgery, major psychological trauma, or significant caloric restriction) can trigger telogen effluvium — a temporary widespread shedding that typically resolves within 3–6 months as the stressor passes. Chronic stress may worsen androgenetic alopecia through cortisol's effects on follicle cycling, but is not itself the primary cause of FPHL.

What is the difference between FPHL and telogen effluvium?

FPHL is a gradual, chronic, progressive condition driven by follicle miniaturisation. Telogen effluvium is an acute or subacute diffuse shedding triggered by a systemic stressor that occurred 2–4 months earlier. Telogen effluvium typically reverses once the trigger resolves; FPHL does not.
