    Feeding Your Brain for the Long Run

    Your brain is only about 2% of your body weight but uses roughly 20% of your energy, and it's exquisitely sensitive to what you feed it — both day to day and across decades. The good news is that the diet linked to better long-term brain health isn't exotic or restrictive. It's the MIND diet, a practical blend of the Mediterranean and DASH patterns developed by researchers at Rush University specifically to protect cognition. In long-running observational studies, people who followed it most closely showed slower memory decline. And because it overlaps almost perfectly with heart-healthy eating, every improvement you make pays off twice.

    What Your Score Means

    Brain-Friendly
    Your diet already lines up closely with the brain-protective pattern. Keep it varied and colourful, and stay consistent — this is a long game.
    Good, Some Gaps
    A strong base with a few brain foods to add. Small, specific additions (berries, greens, fish) give the most return.
    Needs Work
    Several brain-supportive foods are missing. Prioritise the flagged areas — often greens, fish, and cutting fried/processed food.
    Priority
    Multiple gaps and likely a lot of processed food. These are very fixable, and the brain (and heart) benefits of shifting the pattern are substantial.

    The Highest-Impact Additions

    1. Green, leafy, daily. A daily serving of leafy greens is one of the most consistent signals in the brain-health research. Add a handful of spinach to eggs, smoothies, or bowls.
    2. Fish twice a week. Oily fish supplies DHA, the omega-3 that makes up a large share of brain-cell membranes. Not a fish eater? An algae-based omega-3 is a vegan option.
    3. Berries and colour. Berries deliver polyphenols repeatedly linked to memory. In general, the more colours on your plate, the broader the antioxidant coverage.
    4. Swap the fats and grains. Make extra-virgin olive oil your default fat and choose whole grains over refined — simple swaps with outsized long-term payoff.
    5. Mind B12 if plant-based. B12 is essential for nerves and comes almost entirely from animal foods; if you eat little or none, a supplement isn't optional — it's necessary.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What's the best diet for brain health?
    The MIND diet has the strongest evidence — leafy greens, vegetables, berries, nuts, whole grains, beans, fish, poultry and olive oil, with red meat, butter, fried food and sweets limited. It closely mirrors heart-healthy eating.
    Which nutrients matter most?
    Omega-3 (DHA), B vitamins (B12, folate), antioxidants and polyphenols from colourful produce and berries, vitamin E from nuts, and olive oil's monounsaturated fat. But the overall pattern beats any single nutrient or pill.
    Can diet prevent dementia?
    No diet can guarantee prevention, and most evidence is observational. But brain-healthy patterns are linked to slower decline and carry no downside, working alongside sleep, exercise, not smoking, and blood-pressure and blood-sugar control.
    Do brain supplements help?
    Rarely, for people who already eat reasonably — most are overhyped. Correcting a true deficiency (like B12) does matter. Whole foods and overall pattern beat pills in almost every case.
