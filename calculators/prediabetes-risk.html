    What is prediabetes?

    Prediabetes means your blood sugar levels are higher than normal but not yet high enough to be diagnosed as type 2 diabetes. Your pancreas is still producing insulin, but your cells are becoming resistant to it — so glucose builds up in your blood instead of entering your cells.

    It's not a life sentence. With the right lifestyle changes, most people with prediabetes can prevent or significantly delay the development of type 2 diabetes — and some can reverse it entirely. The key is catching it early.

    What the blood sugar numbers mean

    If you've had a blood test, here's how to interpret your glucose results:

    TestNormalPrediabetesDiabetes
    Fasting glucose Below 100 100–125 mg/dL 126+ mg/dL
    2-hr after glucose drink Below 140 140–199 mg/dL 200+ mg/dL
    HbA1c (3-month average) Below 5.7% 5.7% – 6.4% 6.5% or above

    Source: American Diabetes Association Standards of Care 2024.

    Signs prediabetes may have progressed

    Prediabetes itself is usually silent. These signs may suggest your blood sugar has been elevated for some time:

    Increased thirst and frequent urination
    Fatigue that isn't explained by sleep
    Blurred vision or difficulty focusing
    Darkened skin in neck, armpits, or groin (acanthosis nigricans)
    Cuts or bruises that heal slowly
    Tingling or numbness in hands or feet
    Unexplained weight loss
    Recurring infections (skin, gum, bladder)

    Understanding Your Prediabetes Risk Score

    Prediabetes is defined by blood glucose levels that are above normal but below the diagnostic threshold for diabetes — specifically, a fasting blood glucose of 100–125 mg/dL or an HbA1c of 5.7–6.4%. Approximately 96 million American adults have prediabetes — and more than 80% of them don't know it. This matters enormously because prediabetes is the critical intervention window: without lifestyle change, 15–30% of people with prediabetes will progress to type 2 diabetes within 5 years. But with structured intervention, that progression can be prevented in the majority of cases. This screener uses the same validated risk factors used in the ADA's Prediabetes Risk Test to estimate your likelihood of having undetected prediabetes.

    What Your Score Means

    Low Risk
    Low score range
    Few major risk factors for prediabetes are present. Maintaining healthy weight, regular physical activity, and limiting processed sugars remain the most protective ongoing behaviours.
    Moderate Risk
    Mid score range
    Some significant risk factors are present. A fasting glucose and HbA1c test are worthwhile to establish your current status and create a baseline for monitoring.
    High Risk
    High score range
    Multiple significant risk factors are present. The ADA recommends testing for prediabetes in anyone with a high-risk profile. A fasting glucose and HbA1c test, followed by discussion with your doctor, is the essential next step.

    What to Do Next

    1. Get tested — prediabetes has no symptoms. Unlike type 2 diabetes which often causes symptoms, prediabetes is almost always asymptomatic. The only way to know if you have it is a blood test. Fasting glucose and HbA1c together are the standard diagnostic panel.
    2. If you have prediabetes, enrol in a CDC-recognised prevention programme. The CDC's National Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP) delivers the same lifestyle intervention (modest weight loss + 150 minutes of weekly exercise) that was proven in the landmark DPP trial to reduce diabetes progression by 58%. These programmes are widely available in-person and online.
    3. Focus on losing 5–7% of body weight if you're above a healthy weight. This specific amount — not dramatic weight loss — was the threshold in the DPP trial that produced the maximum risk reduction. For a 90 kg person, this is just 4.5–6.3 kg.
    4. Reduce ultra-processed carbohydrates rather than all carbohydrates. The research on dietary patterns and prediabetes shows that it is specifically refined grains, added sugars, and ultra-processed foods that drive glucose dysregulation — not whole food carbohydrates like legumes, whole grains, and fruit.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Can prediabetes be reversed?

    Yes, in the majority of cases through lifestyle intervention. The DPP trial demonstrated that 58% of high-risk individuals with impaired glucose tolerance who completed the lifestyle programme did not progress to diabetes over 3 years. A significant proportion return to completely normal glucose levels.

    Is prediabetes the same as insulin resistance?

    They are closely related but not identical. Insulin resistance is the underlying metabolic mechanism — cells not responding efficiently to insulin. Prediabetes is the blood glucose consequence when insulin resistance is severe enough that the pancreas can no longer fully compensate. You can have insulin resistance without yet meeting prediabetes criteria on fasting glucose or HbA1c.

    Does family history of diabetes mean I will get it?

    Family history is a significant risk factor — having a first-degree relative with type 2 diabetes roughly doubles your lifetime risk. However, genetics is not destiny. The DPP trial showed that lifestyle intervention was effective even in the highest genetic risk participants. Positive family history is a reason to prioritise prevention, not a predetermined outcome.

    How does gestational diabetes relate to prediabetes?

    Women who had gestational diabetes have a 50% lifetime risk of developing type 2 diabetes, most commonly within 10 years of delivery. Post-delivery blood sugar screening and ongoing annual monitoring are recommended for all women with a history of gestational diabetes. The postpartum period is a high-leverage window for lifestyle intervention.

    What is the best diet to reverse prediabetes?

    Multiple dietary patterns have evidence: Mediterranean diet (best overall evidence base), lower-carbohydrate diets (effective for rapid blood glucose improvement), DASH diet, and plant-forward dietary patterns. The consistent common thread is reducing ultra-processed food, increasing fibre, and minimising added sugars. The best diet is the one you can sustain.
