    Symptoms & when to see a doctor

    Early prostate cancer usually causes no symptoms. Urinary symptoms are far more often benign enlargement (BPH), but still worth discussing:

    Weak or interrupted urine stream
    Needing to urinate often, especially at night
    Difficulty starting or trouble emptying
    Blood in urine or semen
    New pain in hips, back, or pelvis
    Unexplained weight loss

    Understanding Your Prostate Cancer Risk

    Prostate cancer occupies an unusual place in medicine: it is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in men, yet the majority of men who have it will never die from it — many prostate cancers grow so slowly they would never have caused harm in a man's lifetime. That paradox is exactly why screening is framed as a personal decision rather than a blanket recommendation. The PSA blood test can catch aggressive cancers early, when they're curable, but it can also find slow ones that lead to biopsies and treatment a man never needed. This calculator helps you understand where you sit on the risk spectrum so that conversation with your doctor starts from the right place.

    What Your Risk Level Means

    Lower Risk
    Few major risk factors. Follow the standard timing — begin discussing PSA screening with your doctor around age 50.
    Moderate
    Some meaningful risk factors. Worth starting the screening conversation a little earlier and knowing your family history well.
    High
    Multiple risk factors, likely including ancestry or family history. Begin the PSA discussion earlier — around 45 — and revisit it regularly.
    Very High
    Strong family history or a hereditary gene. Guidelines suggest starting the screening conversation as early as 40. Bring this profile to your doctor.

    What to Do Next

    1. Know your family history. A father or brother with prostate cancer roughly doubles your risk, and a relative diagnosed young matters more. Ask your relatives — it directly changes when you should start screening.
    2. Have the shared-decision conversation at the right age. Average risk: around 50. Higher risk (Black men, or one close relative): around 45. Highest risk (multiple relatives or BRCA): around 40.
    3. Understand the PSA trade-off. Screening reduces the chance of dying from prostate cancer but can lead to over-diagnosis. A good doctor will help you weigh it for your situation — it's genuinely a personal choice.
    4. Don't rely on symptoms. Early prostate cancer is silent; urinary symptoms are usually benign enlargement. But report blood in the urine, or new hip/back/pelvic pain, promptly.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    At what age should I start screening?
    Average-risk men should discuss PSA screening with their doctor around 50. Higher-risk men — Black men or those with a father/brother affected — should start around 45, and men with multiple affected relatives or a BRCA gene around 40. Routine screening is generally not advised after 70.
    What is the PSA test?
    A blood test measuring prostate-specific antigen. A higher level can signal cancer but is also raised by benign enlargement, infection, or recent activity — so it's imperfect. Because it can lead to unnecessary tests and treatment, whether to test is a personal decision made with your doctor.
    Do urinary symptoms mean cancer?
    Usually not — a weak stream or night-time urination is far more often benign prostate enlargement (BPH). Early prostate cancer typically causes no symptoms, which is why screening is risk-based. Still, mention any new urinary symptoms, and report blood in urine/semen promptly.
    Why are Black men at higher risk?
    Men of African ancestry have higher incidence, tend to develop it younger, and have higher mortality — partly genetic, partly access to care. Guidelines therefore recommend starting the screening conversation earlier, around 45 (or 40 with other risk factors).
