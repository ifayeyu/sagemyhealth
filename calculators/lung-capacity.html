About lung capacity

Lung capacity is the maximum amount of air your lungs can hold. The two key clinical measurements are FVC (Forced Vital Capacity — total air exhaled after a full breath) and FEV1 (Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 second — air exhaled in the first second). Together they reveal both the volume and efficiency of your airways. This calculator uses the Knudson reference equations — the same population-based equations used in clinical spirometry to estimate expected values for your age, height, and sex.

Understanding Your Lung Capacity Results

Pulmonary function testing measures how efficiently your lungs move air in and out, and how well oxygen and carbon dioxide are exchanged. The two key values estimated here — FVC (Forced Vital Capacity) and FEV1 (Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 second) — are the foundation of clinical spirometry, the standard test used to diagnose and monitor asthma, COPD, and other respiratory conditions. Your results are compared to predicted values based on your age, sex, height, and ethnicity, generating a percent-predicted score. A value of 80% predicted is the lower threshold of normal in most clinical guidelines. Beyond their diagnostic role, FEV1 and FVC are powerful predictors of all-cause mortality in longitudinal studies — better lung function at any age is associated with longer, healthier life.

What Your Score Means

Normal

Mild Reduction

Moderate Reduction
50–69% predicted
Significant reduction in ventilatory capacity. Medical evaluation is recommended, including spirometry, history, and assessment for asthma, COPD, or restrictive lung disease.
Severe Reduction
<50% predicted
Substantially impaired lung function. Clinical evaluation and management are essential. This level significantly affects exercise capacity and quality of life.

What to Do Next

  1. If you suspect abnormal results, get a formal spirometry test. Calculator-based estimates cannot replace clinical spirometry, which measures flow-volume loops under standardised conditions and includes bronchodilator reversibility testing — the key step that distinguishes asthma from COPD.
  2. If you smoke, stop — the single most effective intervention for lung function. FEV1 declines at approximately 30–35 mL per year in healthy non-smokers; in susceptible smokers this accelerates to 60–100 mL per year. Cessation at any age significantly slows the rate of decline.
  3. Aerobic exercise consistently improves respiratory function. Cardiorespiratory training increases respiratory muscle strength, tidal volume, and efficiency of gas exchange. Endurance athletes have FVC values 20–40% above age-matched sedentary individuals.
  4. Monitor indoor air quality. Chronic indoor pollutants — from cooking fumes, mould, VOCs from building materials, and second-hand smoke — contribute meaningfully to respiratory impairment. HEPA filtration and ventilation are evidence-based interventions for lung health in high-exposure environments.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between FVC and FEV1?

FVC (Forced Vital Capacity) is the total amount of air you can forcibly exhale after a full inhalation. FEV1 (Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 second) is how much of that air you exhale in the first second. The FEV1/FVC ratio distinguishes obstructive (low ratio, as in COPD and asthma) from restrictive (normal or high ratio with low absolute values) lung disease.

Can lung capacity be improved?

To a meaningful extent, yes. Aerobic training increases respiratory muscle strength and efficiency. Diaphragmatic breathing exercises improve tidal volume. Maintaining a healthy weight reduces the mechanical load on the respiratory system. Avoiding smoking and airway irritants prevents decline. However, structural lung damage (emphysema, pulmonary fibrosis) is largely irreversible once established.

Does altitude affect lung function measurements?

Yes. At high altitude, reduced atmospheric oxygen pressure decreases oxygen saturation but spirometry values (FVC, FEV1) are not directly affected. However, altitude-related respiratory adaptations — increased breathing rate and tidal volume — mean you may feel breathless at levels of exertion that are effortless at sea level, even with completely healthy lungs.

Is reduced lung capacity always due to a disease?

No. Normal aging produces a gradual, predictable decline in FVC and FEV1 — approximately 25–30 mL per year after the peak in the mid-20s. Short stature, some ethnic backgrounds (which is why ethnic-specific reference equations are used), and physically small frame size also produce lower absolute values that are normal for that individual.

What respiratory conditions can a lung function test detect?

Spirometry is the primary diagnostic tool for asthma, COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), restrictive lung diseases (including pulmonary fibrosis), and occupational lung diseases. It is also used to assess surgical fitness, monitor treatment response, and screen high-risk individuals (heavy smokers over 40).
