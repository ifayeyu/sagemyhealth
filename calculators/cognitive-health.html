    What is the Cognitive Health Score?

    This score measures self-reported cognitive function across four clinically recognised domains: memory and recall, sustained attention, processing speed, and mental energy. These are the same domains assessed in neuropsychological evaluations, adapted here as a self-screening questionnaire.

    A lower score doesn't mean something is clinically wrong — it reflects how your cognition is performing today. Poor sleep, high stress, low hydration, and nutrient deficiencies are the most common and reversible causes of reduced scores.

    What Your Cognitive Health Score Reveals

    Cognitive health encompasses far more than memory. Attention, executive function, processing speed, language fluency, and visuospatial reasoning are all distinct domains that age differently, decline at different rates, and respond to different interventions. This calculator assesses the domains most sensitive to early change — the ones where subtle shifts often appear years before any formal diagnosis. The goal is not to alarm you with an early number, but to give you a baseline that is genuinely useful: something to compare against over time, and something that can motivate the evidence-based protective behaviours that are still vastly underutilised even by people who know better.

    What to Do Next

    1. Track your score over time, not just once. A single cognitive assessment is a snapshot. Its power comes from tracking across months and years — identifying whether you are stable, improving with interventions, or showing a trend that warrants clinical evaluation.
    2. Optimise the six lifestyle factors with the strongest cognitive evidence. Aerobic exercise, sleep (7–9 hours), blood pressure control, management of depression, reduced alcohol, and social engagement each independently and additively reduce cognitive decline risk.
    3. Review your medications with your doctor. Many commonly prescribed medications — including antihistamines, sleep aids, certain antidepressants, and bladder medications — have anticholinergic effects that measurably impair cognitive function, particularly in older adults.
    4. If you scored in the reduced range, seek a formal evaluation. Many treatable conditions — depression, hypothyroidism, vitamin B12 deficiency, sleep apnoea, and medication effects — can mimic or contribute to cognitive decline and are not detected by lifestyle-based screeners.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Is this calculator a dementia test?

    No. This calculator assesses lifestyle and self-reported cognitive function — it does not diagnose dementia or mild cognitive impairment (MCI). Formal diagnosis requires comprehensive neuropsychological testing, clinical history, and often neuroimaging or biomarker testing administered by a qualified clinician.

    Does education level affect cognitive test performance?

    Yes, significantly. Higher educational attainment is associated with greater cognitive reserve — more neural networks available to compensate for early damage. This means highly educated individuals may perform better on screening tests even in the presence of early pathology. Clinical testing accounts for education in its scoring norms.

    At what age should I start monitoring cognitive health?

    The research suggests meaningful preventive interventions are most impactful in midlife (40s–60s) — decades before symptoms typically appear. The biological changes underlying Alzheimer's disease, for example, begin 15–20 years before any clinical symptoms. Starting healthy habits and baseline tracking earlier produces greater benefit.

    Can cognitive decline be reversed?

    Decline caused by reversible conditions (depression, medication effects, B12 deficiency, sleep apnoea) can often be substantially reversed with treatment. Neurodegenerative decline is currently not reversible, but its rate can be meaningfully slowed. Early intervention, when the disease burden is still relatively low, is the highest-value window.

    Is crossword puzzles and brain training effective for cognitive health?

    Specific brain training improves performance on the trained task but shows limited transfer to broader cognitive function — a limitation well established in the literature. Aerobic exercise, by contrast, improves performance across multiple untrained cognitive domains. Novel, complex skill learning (an instrument, a language) shows the best evidence for generalised cognitive benefit.
