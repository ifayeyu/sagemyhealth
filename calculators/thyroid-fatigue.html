What you can do right now:

    🚨 Please mention these tests to your doctor:

    TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) — the primary screening test. If abnormal, your doctor may also order Free T4 and, in some cases, Free T3 or thyroid antibodies (TPO-Ab, TgAb) to check for Hashimoto's thyroiditis, the most common cause of hypothyroidism.

    What is thyroid fatigue?

    The thyroid — a butterfly-shaped gland at the base of your neck — produces hormones that regulate every cell's energy production. When it's underactive (hypothyroidism), metabolism slows across the entire body: energy drops, temperature falls, digestion slows, and mood can sink. The fatigue is distinctive — deep, persistent, and unrelieved by sleep.

    Hypothyroidism affects roughly 5% of the population, and up to 10% over age 60. Women are 5–8× more likely to develop it than men. Many cases go undiagnosed for years because the symptoms are vague and overlap with depression, stress, and ageing.

    Understanding Your Thyroid Fatigue Symptom Score

    The thyroid gland — a small butterfly-shaped structure at the base of the neck — produces hormones (T3 and T4) that regulate the metabolic rate of virtually every cell in the body. When thyroid output is insufficient (hypothyroidism), cellular energy production slows across all systems: the cardiovascular system, digestive system, nervous system, and musculoskeletal system. The result is a constellation of symptoms that are often insidious in onset, frequently normalised by patients as ageing or stress, and among the most commonly missed diagnoses in primary care. Hypothyroidism affects approximately 5% of the general population — but subclinical hypothyroidism (where TSH is elevated but T4 is still normal) may affect an additional 5–10%, particularly in older women. This screener estimates the likelihood that thyroid dysfunction is contributing to your symptoms.

    What to Do Next

    1. Request a TSH test from your doctor as the first step. TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) is the most sensitive indicator of thyroid function. An elevated TSH indicates the pituitary is working harder than normal to stimulate a flagging thyroid — the earliest detectable signal of hypothyroidism. TSH rises before T4 falls.
    2. If TSH is abnormal, request free T4 and anti-TPO antibodies. Free T4 (the active thyroid hormone) confirms whether hypothyroidism is clinical or subclinical. Anti-TPO (thyroid peroxidase) antibodies are elevated in 90% of Hashimoto's thyroiditis — the autoimmune condition that is the most common cause of hypothyroidism in iodine-sufficient countries.
    3. Be aware that TSH within the laboratory reference range does not rule out symptom burden. The standard reference range for TSH (typically 0.4–4.0 mIU/L) captures the middle 95% of a mixed reference population. Some patients feel significantly better at TSH values in the lower half of the range. If symptoms persist despite a normal TSH, this nuance is worth discussing with your provider.
    4. Address iodine and selenium status if you are at risk for deficiency. Iodine is essential for thyroid hormone synthesis. Selenium is required for T4-to-T3 conversion. Both deficiencies are rare in Western populations on varied diets but are more common in those following very restrictive dietary patterns or living in iodine-depleted soil regions.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What is the most common cause of hypothyroidism?

    In iodine-sufficient countries, Hashimoto's thyroiditis (autoimmune thyroiditis) is the most common cause, accounting for over 90% of cases. In this condition, the immune system mistakenly attacks thyroid tissue, gradually reducing hormone output. It is far more common in women (estimated 7:1 female-to-male ratio) and has a strong genetic component.

    Can hypothyroidism cause depression?

    Yes. Thyroid hormones directly regulate serotonin and dopamine synthesis and receptor sensitivity. Hypothyroidism is a recognised but frequently missed cause of treatment-resistant depression. The American Psychiatric Association recommends thyroid function testing in all patients presenting with depressive symptoms that do not respond to standard antidepressant therapy.

    What is the difference between hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism?

    Hypothyroidism is underactive thyroid — insufficient hormone production leading to slowed metabolism. Symptoms: fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, hair loss, slow heart rate, depression. Hyperthyroidism is overactive thyroid — excess hormone production accelerating metabolism. Symptoms: anxiety, weight loss, heat intolerance, rapid heartbeat, tremor, insomnia, diarrhoea.

    How is hypothyroidism treated?

    Levothyroxine (synthetic T4) is the standard first-line treatment and is one of the most commonly prescribed medications worldwide. It is taken once daily, typically in the morning on an empty stomach. Dose is adjusted based on TSH response. Some patients with continued symptoms despite normal TSH on levothyroxine benefit from the addition of liothyronine (T3) — a topic of active research and clinical debate.

    Can the thyroid affect hair loss?

    Yes, significantly. Both hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism can cause diffuse hair shedding (telogen effluvium) and changes in hair texture and quality. Hair follicles have direct thyroid hormone receptors, and disrupted thyroid signalling impairs normal follicle cycling. Hair loss from thyroid dysfunction typically reverses within 6–12 months of achieving and maintaining optimal thyroid hormone levels.
