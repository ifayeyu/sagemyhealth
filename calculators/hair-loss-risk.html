What causes hair loss?

Hair loss is multifactorial — genetics accounts for the most common types (androgenetic alopecia affects roughly 50% of people by age 50), but hormones, nutrition, stress, and scalp health all play significant roles. Iron deficiency is one of the most common and overlooked causes, especially in women. Telogen effluvium — shedding triggered by physical or emotional stress — is usually temporary. Understanding your personal risk profile is the first step toward targeted action.

Understanding Your Hair Loss Risk Score

Hair loss is among the most common concerns seen in both dermatology and primary care, yet it is also one of the most frequently dismissed as inevitable. The reality is more nuanced: while androgenetic alopecia (the hereditary variety) follows a largely genetic course, multiple contributing and modifiable factors determine whether that genetic predisposition is expressed early, late, or dramatically. This screener evaluates your combined risk profile across the dimensions that most predict hair loss onset and progression — genetics, hormonal signals, nutritional status, stress load, scalp health, and hairstyling practices. The goal is to give you an actionable risk picture before clinically significant loss is visible.

What to Do Next

  1. Establish a baseline now with photos. Hair loss is gradual and easy to miss until it is advanced. Consistent overhead and parting-line photos taken in identical lighting conditions every 3 months give you an objective, comparative record that is far more useful than memory.
  2. Check your ferritin level. Iron deficiency — particularly low ferritin, which reflects stored iron — is one of the most common and treatable contributors to non-genetic hair shedding. Most dermatologists recommend a ferritin level above 70 ng/mL for optimal hair cycling. Standard lab reference ranges are often set lower.
  3. Evaluate and modify any high-traction hairstyling practices. Tight braids, weaves, high ponytails, and chemical relaxers applied repeatedly to the same scalp area cause traction alopecia — a form of hair loss that starts reversible but can become permanent if the traction is sustained for years.
  4. If you have a strong family history, consider an early dermatology consultation. Finasteride (in men), dutasteride, spironolactone (in women), and minoxidil all have stronger effects when started before significant follicle miniaturisation has occurred.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hair loss inherited from the mother or the father?

Both. The genetics of androgenetic alopecia are polygenic — multiple genes from both parents contribute. The AR gene (androgen receptor) on the X chromosome is one important factor, which is why maternal grandfather's hair is sometimes used as a proxy, but paternal history is equally relevant.

How much daily hair shedding is normal?

Losing 50–100 hairs per day is within the normal range — the scalp contains approximately 100,000 follicles that cycle through growth, transition, and resting phases. Temporary increases above this during high-stress periods or seasonal changes (September and March show spikes in many population studies) are typically normal.

Do hair growth supplements work?

The ingredients with the best evidence are: iron (for deficient individuals), zinc (for deficient individuals), and saw palmetto (modest anti-androgenic effect with limited evidence). Biotin, collagen, and most proprietary blends have weak or no clinical evidence in non-deficient populations. The supplement market for hair growth is largely outpacing the evidence.

Can scalp massage stimulate hair growth?

A small but intriguing clinical trial (Koyama et al., 2016) found that 4 minutes of standardised daily scalp massage over 24 weeks increased hair thickness in male subjects. The proposed mechanism is mechanical stretching of dermal papilla cells, which stimulates hair growth signalling. The evidence remains preliminary.

Is PRP (platelet-rich plasma) therapy effective for hair loss?

PRP has a growing evidence base for androgenetic alopecia in both men and women. Multiple controlled trials show increased hair count and density with 3–4 monthly sessions followed by maintenance treatments. Results vary significantly by individual, and the therapy is not yet FDA-approved specifically for hair loss, though it is FDA-cleared for the equipment used.
