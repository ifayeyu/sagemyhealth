What to do next:

    What is burnout?

    Burnout is a state of chronic stress that leads to physical and emotional exhaustion, cynicism, and feelings of ineffectiveness. It builds gradually from prolonged exposure to demanding work without adequate rest or recovery.

    This assessment uses the two core dimensions of the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory (OLBI): Exhaustion (feeling depleted and drained) and Disengagement (pulling away emotionally from your work and losing your sense of purpose).

    Physical & behavioural signs of burnout

    Burnout often shows up in the body and in behaviour before it's recognised emotionally. Common signs include:

    Constant fatigue, even after sleep
    Frequent headaches or muscle tension
    Getting ill more often than usual
    Difficulty concentrating or making decisions
    Increasing irritability or impatience
    Procrastinating or missing deadlines
    Withdrawing from friends and colleagues
    Feeling like nothing you do makes a difference

    Understanding Your Burnout Risk Score

    Burnout is not a character flaw or a synonym for tiredness. It is a clinically recognised syndrome — formally included in the World Health Organization's ICD-11 — characterised by three dimensions: exhaustion from sustained occupational demands, growing detachment or cynicism toward one's work, and a diminishing sense of personal effectiveness. The Oldenburg Burnout Inventory (OLBI), on which this calculator is based, is one of the most extensively validated burnout measures in occupational psychology, preferred in research settings for its cross-cultural reliability. Your score is not a prediction of collapse — it is a map of where your reserves currently stand, and where targeted recovery would have the most impact.

    What Your Score Means

    Low Risk
    Low exhaustion + Low disengagement
    Your current reserves appear adequate relative to your demands. The goal at this stage is maintenance: protect the habits and relationships that are sustaining you.
    Moderate Risk
    Moderate in one or both dimensions
    Early warning territory. Research shows burnout is far easier to recover from when addressed at this stage than when symptoms become entrenched. This is the intervention window.
    High Risk
    High exhaustion and/or disengagement
    Significant depletion is present. At this level, cognitive performance, immune function, and relationship quality are typically impaired. Structural change — not just self-care — is needed.

    What to Do Next

    1. Separate exhaustion from disengagement. The two OLBI subscales point to different remedies. Exhaustion responds to reducing demands and increasing physical recovery. Disengagement responds better to reconnecting with purpose, autonomy, and mastery within your work.
    2. Identify one concrete workload change you can make this week. Research by Leiter and Maslach shows burnout is driven by six workplace mismatches: workload, control, reward, community, fairness, and values. Identifying which mismatch is most acute for you is more productive than generic stress management.
    3. Talk to someone — your manager, HR, a therapist, or a trusted colleague. Social support is the single most protective factor against burnout relapse in longitudinal studies. Isolation accelerates burnout; connection buffers it.
    4. If you scored high, consider a formal mental health evaluation. Severe burnout overlaps clinically with depression and anxiety disorders, and the treatment for those conditions differs from workplace interventions alone.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Is burnout the same as depression?

    They share symptoms — fatigue, reduced motivation, cognitive difficulty — but they are not identical. Burnout is specifically tied to occupational context and typically improves when work demands change. Clinical depression is pervasive across all life domains. They can co-occur, and distinguishing them is important because the treatments differ.

    How long does it take to recover from burnout?

    Research by Bianchi and colleagues suggests full recovery from severe burnout takes 3–12 months when meaningful changes in workload, support, and recovery are made. Without structural changes — just taking a vacation — most people return to burnout symptoms within weeks.

    Can I prevent burnout while staying in a demanding job?

    Yes, though it requires deliberate management of the six burnout drivers. The most protective factors in demanding careers are: high autonomy, perceived fairness, strong collegial support, and regular opportunities for skill development and impact.

    Does physical exercise actually help burnout?

    The evidence suggests yes. Aerobic exercise reduces inflammatory markers that are elevated in burnout, improves sleep quality, and modulates cortisol reactivity. The key is that exercise should feel restorative — short, enjoyable activity is more protective than forced intense training.

    What is the difference between burnout and ADHD burnout?

    ADHD burnout is a distinct pattern — a crash following a period of intense hyperfocus or sustained masking effort — that reflects the specific neurological demands placed on people with ADHD. It typically involves more pronounced cognitive symptoms and a stronger boom-bust cycle than classical occupational burnout.
