    How many carbs do you actually need?

    There's no single right number — it's a range that flexes with your calories, activity, and preference. The IOM's Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range (AMDR) is the anchor most guidelines use.

    How to Set Your Carbohydrate Intake

    Carbohydrate is the body's most convenient fuel — especially for the brain and for higher-intensity exercise. Yet it's also the macronutrient people worry about most, largely because the conversation has fixated on quantity while ignoring quality. As a starting principle: your total carbohydrate should be set by your calorie needs and how you train, but the type of carbohydrate you choose matters more than the exact gram total for almost everyone. This calculator gives you a personalised target using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation to estimate your calories, then applies your chosen share of carbohydrate within the established 45–65% range (or lower, if you prefer).

    Why the Number Is a Range, Not a Rule

    A marathoner and a desk worker of the same weight have very different carbohydrate needs, because carbohydrate is the fuel that's burned fastest during hard effort. That's why the healthy range is wide. Active people generally feel and perform better toward the higher end; people focused on appetite control or steadier blood sugar often prefer the lower end. Both can be perfectly healthy — the deciding factors are your energy needs, your response, and your ability to stick with it.

    Making Your Carbs Work Harder

    1. Prioritise fibre. Aim for the fibre target above (about 14 grams per 1,000 calories). Most of the health benefit of carbohydrate-rich foods travels with their fibre — whole grains, legumes, fruit, and vegetables.
    2. Choose whole over refined. Swap white bread, white rice, and sugary drinks for their whole-food equivalents. Same macro category, very different effect on energy and fullness.
    3. Time carbs around activity. If you train hard, placing more of your carbohydrate near workouts supports performance and recovery.
    4. Keep protein steady. Whatever your carb level, adequate protein protects muscle and helps with fullness — set that first, then flex carbs and fat around it.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    How many carbs should I eat per day?
    Most adults do well with 45–65% of calories from carbohydrate — roughly 225–325 g on a 2,000-calorie diet. Your ideal number depends on your calorie needs, activity, and goals. Use the calculator above for a personalised figure.
    How many carbs to lose weight?
    Weight loss comes from an overall calorie deficit, not from cutting carbs specifically. A moderate reduction (around 30–40% of calories) helps some people control appetite, but a balanced approach works too. Focus on fibre-rich carbs and adequate protein.
    What are net carbs?
    Net carbs are total carbohydrate minus fibre (and sugar alcohols), used mainly in low-carb and keto diets. For general healthy eating, total carbohydrate and, above all, carb quality matter more.
    Are carbs bad for you?
    No — quality matters far more than quantity. Whole grains, legumes, fruit and vegetables are strongly linked to better health, while refined grains and added sugars are the ones worth limiting.
