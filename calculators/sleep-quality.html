    About the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI)

    The PSQI is a validated clinical questionnaire developed at the University of Pittsburgh and used in sleep research worldwide. It measures seven components of sleep health: subjective quality, latency, duration, efficiency, disturbances, medication use, and daytime dysfunction. A total PSQI score above 5 indicates clinically poor sleep.

    Understanding Your PSQI Sleep Quality Score

    The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was developed at the University of Pittsburgh in 1989 and has since become the most widely used self-report measure of sleep quality in clinical research and practice worldwide. Unlike measures that simply ask how long you sleep, the PSQI assesses seven distinct components of sleep: subjective quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep aids, and daytime dysfunction. This comprehensive approach reflects what sleep scientists know — that sleep quality is multidimensional. You can sleep 8 hours but wake 10 times, take 90 minutes to fall asleep, or experience no slow-wave sleep, and feel just as unrefreshed as if you'd slept only 5 hours. The PSQI captures these distinctions.

    What Your Score Means

    Good Sleep Quality
    PSQI Score 0–5
    Sleep quality is within the clinically normal range. The PSQI validation study by Buysse et al. used a score of 5 as the diagnostic threshold, with good sensitivity and specificity for distinguishing good from poor sleepers.
    Moderate Sleep Problems
    PSQI Score 6–10
    Sleep quality is somewhat compromised. Specific components — sleep latency, disturbances, or daytime dysfunction — are likely contributing. These are the highest-yield areas to target with sleep hygiene interventions.
    Poor Sleep Quality
    PSQI Score 11–15
    Significant sleep quality impairment is present. Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) is the first-line recommended treatment and has stronger evidence than pharmacological sleep aids.
    Severely Poor Sleep
    PSQI Score 16–21
    Severe sleep disturbance across multiple domains. Medical evaluation is recommended to rule out sleep apnoea, restless legs syndrome, periodic limb movement disorder, or conditions requiring specific treatment.

    What to Do Next

    1. Try CBT-I before sleep medications. Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) is recommended by the ACP and AASM as the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia, ahead of medication. It produces durable improvements by addressing the cognitive and behavioural patterns that maintain poor sleep, rather than just sedating. Multiple digital CBT-I programmes (Sleepio, Somryst) are validated and accessible.
    2. Identify which PSQI component is highest for you. Sleep latency problems (taking over 30 minutes to fall asleep) have different interventions than sleep maintenance problems (waking in the night) or early morning awakening (which often relates to depression or anxiety). The component driving your score determines the most useful intervention.
    3. Address bedroom environment systematically. Evidence-based sleep hygiene includes: room temperature of 16–19°C (cool promotes sleep), complete darkness (blackout curtains or eye mask), minimal noise (earplugs or white noise), and reserving the bed for sleep and sex only (to strengthen the psychological association between bed and sleep).
    4. Rule out sleep apnoea if you snore or wake unrefreshed. Obstructive sleep apnoea is the most prevalent undiagnosed sleep disorder, affecting an estimated 25% of adult men and 10% of adult women. It profoundly impairs sleep quality while often leaving total sleep time apparently normal. A home sleep test is now available without needing to spend a night in a sleep lab.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What is the difference between insomnia and poor sleep quality?

    Insomnia is a clinically defined disorder characterised by difficulty falling asleep or staying asleep, occurring at least 3 nights per week for 3 or more months, accompanied by daytime consequences. Poor sleep quality is broader — it includes unrefreshing sleep despite adequate duration, which can result from sleep apnoea, fragmented sleep architecture, or environmental factors, even without classic insomnia complaints.

    Is it normal to wake during the night?

    Brief awakenings during the night are a normal feature of sleep architecture — the transition between sleep cycles (which last approximately 90 minutes) often includes brief waking that is not remembered. What is clinically significant is waking fully, remaining awake for 20 minutes or more, and experiencing distress or daytime consequences.

    Do sleep trackers accurately measure sleep stages?

    Consumer sleep trackers are better than nothing but significantly less accurate than polysomnography (the clinical standard). A 2019 study found that popular wearables accurately detect sleep vs wakefulness roughly 90% of the time but are much less reliable at distinguishing specific sleep stages (REM vs deep sleep). They are most useful for tracking trends over time rather than absolute nightly stage values.

    What medications most affect sleep quality?

    Common medications that impair sleep quality include: beta-blockers (suppress REM sleep), SSRIs and SNRIs (delay and reduce REM), corticosteroids (cause insomnia), stimulants, certain antihypertensives, diuretics (nocturnal urination), and some antihistamines and decongestants. If you recently started a medication and your sleep worsened, review it with your prescriber.

    Does alcohol improve or harm sleep?

    Alcohol's sedative effect speeds sleep onset (which people often interpret as improving sleep) but disrupts sleep quality by: suppressing REM sleep in the first half of the night, causing rebound arousal in the second half as alcohol is metabolised, worsening snoring and sleep apnoea, and increasing nocturnal urination. Net effect on sleep quality is consistently negative in controlled studies.
