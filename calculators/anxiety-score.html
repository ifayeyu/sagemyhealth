    About the GAD-7

    The GAD-7 is a seven-item self-report questionnaire that screens for and measures the severity of generalized anxiety disorder. It was developed by researchers at Columbia University and has been validated in numerous studies. A score of 10 or higher suggests clinically significant anxiety. This tool is not a diagnosis—it's a starting point for conversations with a healthcare provider.

    Understanding Your GAD-7 Anxiety Score

    The GAD-7 is one of the most rigorously validated mental health screening tools in clinical medicine. Developed at Columbia University and now used in physician offices, hospitals, and research trials worldwide, it was designed to quantify the severity of generalized anxiety disorder — not simply to classify anxiety as present or absent, but to give you and your care team a precise, reproducible number that can be tracked over time. Your score today is a snapshot, not a sentence. It tells you where you are right now and, crucially, what kind of support is likely to help.

    What Your Score Means

    Minimal
    Score 0–4
    Anxiety symptoms are unlikely to be interfering with daily functioning. No clinical intervention is typically indicated.
    Mild
    Score 5–9
    Some anxiety present, may occasionally disrupt sleep or concentration. Self-care strategies and monitoring are appropriate first steps.
    Moderate
    Score 10–14
    A clinically meaningful level. The original GAD-7 validation study found this range correlates with significant functional impairment.
    Severe
    Score 15–21
    Symptoms are substantial. Clinical trials use this threshold to identify the population most likely to benefit from therapy, medication, or both.

    What to Do Next

    1. Log your score and the date. The GAD-7 is most powerful as a trend — clinicians retake it every 2–4 weeks to track whether symptoms are improving, stable, or worsening.
    2. If your score is 10 or above, talk to a doctor or therapist. A score in the moderate or severe range does not automatically mean you need medication, but it does mean a professional evaluation is warranted — opening the door to evidence-based treatments including cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT).
    3. Examine your lifestyle inputs. Caffeine amplifies anxiety, poor sleep exacerbates it, and regular aerobic exercise has been shown in controlled trials to reduce GAD symptoms comparably to first-line medications for mild-to-moderate cases.
    4. If you scored severe and are having thoughts of self-harm, reach out now. The 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988) and the Crisis Text Line (text HOME to 741741) are available around the clock.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Is the GAD-7 the same as a diagnosis of anxiety disorder?

    No. The GAD-7 is a screening tool, not a diagnostic instrument. A high score signals that further evaluation is warranted; only a qualified clinician can make a formal diagnosis of generalized anxiety disorder or any related condition.

    Can my GAD-7 score change from day to day?

    Yes, and that is expected. The instructions ask you to reflect on the past two weeks — not just today — precisely to smooth out daily fluctuation. A clinically meaningful signal is a sustained shift of 5 or more points.

    What is the difference between anxiety and normal worry?

    Worry is universal. Anxiety disorder is characterised by worry that is excessive, difficult to control, accompanied by physical symptoms (restlessness, muscle tension, sleep disruption), and persistent for at least six months, per DSM-5 criteria. The GAD-7 captures all of these dimensions.

    Can I use my GAD-7 score to track whether therapy is working?

    Absolutely — this is one of its primary clinical uses. Many therapists administer the GAD-7 at every session. A reduction of 5 or more points is considered a clinically significant response; a score below 5 is considered remission.

    What should I do if my partner or child scores high?

    Encourage them to share the result with a healthcare provider rather than acting on the score alone. Offering to accompany them to an appointment and helping them identify trusted providers are the most effective practical steps you can take.
