How to measure your waist

Stand relaxed (don't suck in). Place the tape horizontally around your natural waist — the narrowest part of your torso, usually halfway between your lowest rib and the top of your hip bone. Measure at the end of a normal exhale. Take two readings and average them.

What to do next:

    Why waist-to-height ratio matters

    Unlike BMI, which doesn't distinguish between muscle and fat or where fat is stored, waist-to-height ratio directly measures central adiposity — the fat that accumulates around your abdomen and visceral organs. This fat is metabolically active: it releases inflammatory compounds and is strongly linked to insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and hypertension.

    Research suggests WHtR is a better predictor of cardiometabolic risk than BMI, particularly for people who are "normal weight obese" — a healthy BMI but excess central fat.

    How WHtR compares to BMI

    WHtR advantages

    Captures central fat distribution. Works across different heights and ethnicities. Simple one-number rule (keep below 0.5). Better predictor of metabolic risk.

    BMI advantages

    Widely used in clinical practice and research. Doesn't require measuring waist circumference. More data available for population comparison.

    Understanding Your Waist-to-Height Ratio

    The waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) has emerged over the past two decades as one of the most powerful simple measures of cardiometabolic risk — more predictive of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and metabolic syndrome than BMI alone. Unlike BMI, which reflects total body mass relative to height, WHtR specifically captures abdominal adiposity — the accumulation of visceral fat around the abdominal organs that is metabolically distinct from subcutaneous fat. Visceral fat is not inert tissue; it is endocrinologically active, secreting inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) and disrupting insulin signalling in ways that directly drive cardiometabolic disease. The simple rule: keep your waist circumference to less than half your height.

    What Your Score Means

    Healthy
    WHtR < 0.50
    Waist circumference is proportionate to height. Cardiometabolic risk from abdominal adiposity is within the healthy range. Maintaining this ratio is one of the most direct protective measures for cardiovascular health.
    Increased Risk
    WHtR 0.50–0.59
    Visceral adiposity is above the optimal range. Cardiometabolic risk is measurably elevated. Interventions targeting central fat — aerobic exercise and dietary quality — directly address this measure.
    High Risk
    WHtR 0.60–0.69
    Substantial excess central adiposity. A large meta-analysis by Ashwell et al. found WHtR in this range associated with approximately 2–3 times the risk of cardiovascular events compared to the healthy range.
    Very High Risk
    WHtR ≥ 0.70
    Severe central adiposity. This level is associated with very high risk of type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. Medical evaluation is strongly recommended.

    What to Do Next

    1. Measure your waist correctly for an accurate WHtR. Measure at the midpoint between the bottom of the lowest rib and the top of the hip bone (iliac crest), which is approximately at the level of the navel for most people. Take the measurement at the end of a normal exhale, with the tape parallel to the floor and snug but not compressing the skin.
    2. Prioritise aerobic exercise as the most effective intervention for visceral fat. Visceral fat responds more readily to aerobic exercise than subcutaneous fat. Research consistently shows that regular cardiorespiratory training reduces visceral fat independent of total weight loss — even when body weight stays roughly the same.
    3. Address dietary quality rather than just caloric quantity. Ultra-processed foods, high-fructose sweeteners (particularly from beverages), trans fats, and excessive alcohol are especially strongly associated with visceral fat accumulation. The Mediterranean and DASH dietary patterns are associated with lower WHtR in population studies.
    4. Track WHtR rather than BMI as your primary health metric going forward. Because WHtR is more sensitive to changes in visceral fat and less confounded by muscle mass, it is a more clinically informative indicator of metabolic improvement than BMI. A decrease in WHtR even without significant scale weight change is a meaningful sign of improving metabolic health.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Is WHtR better than BMI?

    For predicting cardiometabolic risk specifically, most comparative studies conclude yes. A 2012 meta-analysis of 31 studies by Ashwell et al. found WHtR superior to BMI and waist circumference alone for predicting diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular events, and metabolic syndrome. WHtR also applies equally across ethnic groups and sexes without separate thresholds.

    Why is visceral fat more dangerous than fat elsewhere?

    Visceral fat (fat surrounding abdominal organs, also called intra-abdominal fat) drains directly into the portal circulation, exposing the liver to high concentrations of fatty acids and inflammatory cytokines. It is also more metabolically active than subcutaneous fat — releasing more inflammatory molecules, interfering with insulin signalling, and contributing to non-alcoholic fatty liver disease.

    Does WHtR change with age?

    Waist circumference naturally tends to increase with age even when total weight is stable, partly due to hormonal changes (particularly oestrogen decline in women after menopause and testosterone decline in men) that shift fat distribution toward the abdomen. Maintaining the same WHtR with age therefore requires active intervention.

    Can WHtR improve without losing weight?

    Yes — and this is one of its most clinically important properties. Aerobic exercise redistributes fat from visceral to subcutaneous compartments even when total body weight remains stable. Body recomposition (gaining muscle while losing fat) also reduces WHtR without necessarily moving the scale.

    What is the ideal WHtR for men vs women?

    The primary threshold (0.50) applies to both sexes, which is one of WHtR's advantages over sex-specific waist circumference cutoffs. Some research suggests a slightly higher threshold of 0.53 for men and 0.49 for women better captures risk in each group, but the round 0.50 boundary is used most commonly in clinical guidelines.
