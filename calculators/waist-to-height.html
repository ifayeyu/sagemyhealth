How to measure your waist

Stand relaxed (don't suck in). Place the tape horizontally around your natural waist — the narrowest part of your torso, usually halfway between your lowest rib and the top of your hip bone. Measure at the end of a normal exhale. Take two readings and average them.

What to do next:

    Why waist-to-height ratio matters

    Unlike BMI, which doesn't distinguish between muscle and fat or where fat is stored, waist-to-height ratio directly measures central adiposity — the fat that accumulates around your abdomen and visceral organs. This fat is metabolically active: it releases inflammatory compounds and is strongly linked to insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and hypertension.

    Research suggests WHtR is a better predictor of cardiometabolic risk than BMI, particularly for people who are "normal weight obese" — a healthy BMI but excess central fat.

    How WHtR compares to BMI

    WHtR advantages

    Captures central fat distribution. Works across different heights and ethnicities. Simple one-number rule (keep below 0.5). Better predictor of metabolic risk.

    BMI advantages

    Widely used in clinical practice and research. Doesn't require measuring waist circumference. More data available for population comparison.

    Source: Ashwell M & Hsieh SD (2005). A proposal for a primary screening tool: 'Keep your waist circumference to less than half your height.' BMC Med. Also: Browning LM, Hsieh SD, Ashwell M (2010). Int J Obes, 34(6):1065–79.
