    What is BMI?

    Body Mass Index (BMI) is calculated by dividing your weight (in kilograms) by the square of your height (in metres). It was developed as a population-level screening tool and is widely used by healthcare providers as a starting point — not a final verdict. A high BMI may indicate higher health risk, but it doesn't tell the full story for every individual.

    Understanding Your BMI — and Its Limits

    Body Mass Index was developed by the Belgian mathematician Adolphe Quetelet in the 1830s as a population-level statistical tool — never intended for individual clinical assessment. Yet for nearly two centuries it has been the most widely used proxy for body composition in medicine, public health policy, and research. There is genuine value in knowing your BMI: it correlates with population-level risk for diabetes, hypertension, sleep apnoea, and certain cancers. But it misclassifies millions of people. A muscular athlete may score as overweight, while someone with low muscle mass and high visceral fat may score as normal. Use this number as one data point among several, not as a verdict on your health.

    What Your Score Means

    Underweight
    BMI < 18.5
    Associated with increased risk of bone loss, immune suppression, and nutrient deficiency. A clinician review of dietary intake and underlying causes is recommended.
    Normal Weight
    BMI 18.5–24.9
    Associated with the lowest all-cause mortality in most large population studies. Fitness level and waist circumference remain important additional measures.
    Overweight
    BMI 25.0–29.9
    Modest but measurable increased risk for type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and joint disease. Many people in this range have excellent metabolic markers.
    Obese Class I
    BMI 30.0–34.9
    Risk for cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes increases meaningfully. Even a 5–10% weight reduction produces measurable improvements in metabolic markers.
    Obese Class II–III
    BMI ≥ 35
    Collaboration with a physician or multidisciplinary weight-management team is strongly recommended. Effective evidence-based treatments are available.

    What to Do Next

    1. Pair your BMI with waist circumference. A waist above 35 inches (women) or 40 inches (men) is a stronger independent predictor of cardiovascular risk than BMI alone, because it reflects visceral adiposity rather than total body mass.
    2. Check your metabolic numbers. Fasting glucose, blood pressure, LDL cholesterol, and triglycerides tell a more complete story than any single weight-based metric. Ask your doctor for a metabolic panel at your next visit.
    3. Prioritise fitness alongside weight. A 2021 meta-analysis in British Journal of Sports Medicine found that cardiorespiratory fitness was a stronger predictor of mortality than BMI — meaning a fit person with a high BMI often has better outcomes than an unfit person with a normal BMI.
    4. Avoid BMI-only conversations with your healthcare provider. Ask specifically about metabolic markers, body composition if available, and function-based goals rather than BMI targets alone.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Does BMI apply equally to all ethnicities?

    No. Research consistently shows that people of East Asian and South Asian descent carry higher cardiometabolic risk at lower BMI values. Many clinical guidelines now recommend lowering the overweight threshold to BMI ≥ 23 for these populations.

    Is BMI accurate for athletes and very muscular people?

    Not reliably. Muscle is denser than fat, so a highly muscular person can have a BMI in the overweight range with very little body fat. In these cases, body fat percentage measurement via DEXA gives a far more accurate picture.

    Can children use this calculator?

    This calculator is calibrated for adults. For children and adolescents, BMI is interpreted on a percentile chart adjusted for age and sex. A paediatrician should interpret BMI for anyone under 18.

    What is a realistic, healthy rate of weight change?

    Clinical guidelines generally recommend a weight loss rate of 0.5–1 kg (1–2 lbs) per week for sustained fat loss, achieved through a moderate caloric deficit of 500–750 kcal per day. Faster rates are associated with muscle loss.

    My BMI is normal but I don't feel healthy — what else should I check?

    BMI tells you nothing about cardiovascular fitness, muscle mass, blood sugar regulation, sleep quality, or nutritional status. A comprehensive annual wellness exam is the best way to identify what the scale cannot.
