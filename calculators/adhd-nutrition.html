    What the evidence actually says

    Nutrition is a supporting player in ADHD, not a treatment. But a few areas have genuine evidence behind them, and fixing a real shortfall removes an unnecessary drag on attention.

    Omega-3 (EPA/DHA)Small but real symptom benefit, larger if intake is low.
    Protein & steady carbsBlunt blood-sugar swings that mimic poor concentration.

    Nutrition and Attention: What Helps, What Doesn't

    It's worth being clear up front, because the internet is not: no diet causes ADHD, and no diet cures it. ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition, and the treatments with the strongest evidence are behavioural strategies, coaching, and — where appropriate — medication. What nutrition can do is remove obstacles. An attention system that's already working hard doesn't need the extra handicap of low iron, wild blood-sugar swings, or dehydration. Fix those, and you're not treating ADHD — you're clearing the runway so everything else works a little better. This score focuses on the handful of nutrition factors that actually have evidence behind them.

    Where to Start

    1. Anchor the morning with protein. A breakfast built around protein (eggs, Greek yoghurt, nuts) steadies blood sugar and supplies the building blocks for dopamine — the single highest-leverage change for most people.
    2. Check iron before supplementing. Low iron genuinely worsens attention, but iron is not something to guess at — ask your doctor for a ferritin test, and only supplement if you're actually low.
    3. Smooth the blood-sugar curve. Swap refined carbs and sugary drinks for whole grains, fruit, and protein-paired snacks. The goal is fewer crashes, which so often masquerade as poor concentration.
    4. Add omega-3 and the minerals. Oily fish twice a week (or a quality supplement), plus nuts, seeds, and legumes for zinc and magnesium — low-risk additions with modest, real upside.
    5. Don't forget water. Even mild dehydration dents concentration. A visible water bottle is a simple, effective cue.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Can diet treat ADHD?
    No. Diet doesn't cause or cure ADHD and isn't a substitute for evidence-based treatment. But correcting real nutritional shortfalls — especially low iron and unstable blood sugar — removes things that make focus harder. Nutrition supports; it doesn't treat.
    What's the iron–ADHD link?
    Iron is required to make dopamine, the neurotransmitter central to attention and motivation. Lower ferritin has been linked to worse inattention, which is why checking iron status is often suggested — but only supplement if a blood test confirms you're low.
    Do sugar and additives cause hyperactivity?
    For most people, sugar doesn't directly cause hyperactivity, but the blood-sugar crash after a sugar spike can impair concentration and mood. A small subset of children react to certain artificial colours; if you suspect that, discuss a structured trial with a professional rather than guessing.
    Should I try an elimination diet?
    Restrictive elimination diets can risk nutrient gaps and are hard to sustain, so they're best done only with professional guidance and a clear reason. For most people, the steady wins here — protein, iron, stable blood sugar, omega-3 — matter far more.
