Strategies that tend to help:

    What is executive function?

    Executive functions are the mental control skills coordinated largely by the brain's prefrontal cortex — they're what let you set a goal and actually follow through. Psychologists usually group them into three cores: working memory (holding information in mind), inhibition (resisting distraction and impulse), and cognitive flexibility (shifting between tasks and perspectives). From these grow higher skills like planning, time management, task initiation, and emotional self-regulation.

    Executive function difficulty is the central thread in ADHD, but it isn't unique to it — sleep deprivation, chronic stress, anxiety, depression, and simply being overloaded all tax the same system.

    Making Sense of Your Executive Function Profile

    Think of executive function as the brain's air-traffic control system: it decides what gets your attention, holds the flight plan in mind, sequences the steps, and keeps impulses from crashing the runway. When that system is stretched — by ADHD, by stress, by poor sleep, or simply by too much to manage — capable, intelligent people can find themselves unable to start tasks, losing track mid-thought, or reacting more intensely than they'd like. None of that reflects laziness or lack of willpower. It reflects how much load the control system is carrying relative to its current capacity.

    What Your Score Band Means

    Minimal
    Your executive skills are largely working for you. Occasional lapses are normal — everyone loses their keys sometimes.
    Mild
    Some everyday friction. Simple structure — timers, checklists, fewer distractions — usually makes a real difference.
    Moderate
    Executive difficulties are affecting daily life across several domains. Worth building deliberate systems, and worth a professional conversation.
    Significant
    Substantial, wide-ranging difficulty. A clinical evaluation is a sensible next step — the right support (which may include assessment for ADHD) can change a lot.

    Turning Insight Into Action

    1. Externalise everything. Executive function struggles ease dramatically when the load moves out of your head and onto paper or a screen — calendars, checklists, visible timers, and one capture spot for tasks.
    2. Shrink the first step. Task initiation is often the real barrier. Make the starting step so small it's almost silly ("open the document"), and momentum usually follows.
    3. Protect the basics. Sleep, movement, and stress load directly shape executive capacity. A tired, overloaded brain will always score worse — improving these often lifts every domain at once.
    4. Match support to your weakest domain. Use the breakdown above: if time management is the gap, lean on alarms and time-boxing; if working memory is, write things down immediately; if self-regulation is, build in pauses before responding.
    5. Consider an assessment if it's persistent. Lifelong, cross-situational executive difficulty — especially with a moderate or significant score — is worth discussing with a clinician, who can distinguish ADHD from other causes and open up options like coaching or medication.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Is a low score the same as having ADHD?
    No. Executive dysfunction is central to ADHD but also appears with anxiety, depression, poor sleep, stress, and thyroid issues. This tool measures current difficulty — only a qualified clinician can diagnose ADHD, using your full history and development.
    Can executive function actually improve?
    Yes. It responds well to external scaffolding (timers, lists, routines), to sleep and exercise, and — in ADHD — to medication and coaching. It's a supportable skill set, not a fixed limit.
    Why am I strong in some domains and weak in others?
    Executive function is a cluster of related-but-separate skills. Strong working memory with weak time management (or vice versa) is common. The domain breakdown helps you target support where it counts.
    Should I retake this?
    It can be useful to re-check after changing sleep, stress, or support strategies — or before and after a clinical conversation. Because it's based on the last 6 months, it reflects a period rather than a single day.
