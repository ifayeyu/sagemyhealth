Recommended next steps:

    Who qualifies for lung cancer screening?

    The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (2021) recommends yearly screening with a low-dose CT scan (LDCT) for people who meet all three of the following:

    CriterionThreshold
    Age50 – 80 years
    Smoking history20 pack-years or more
    Current statusStill smoke, or quit within the last 15 years

    Screening can stop once you have not smoked for 15 years, or if a health problem substantially limits life expectancy.

    Symptoms worth checking promptly

    Screening is for people without symptoms. If you have any of these — especially if they persist beyond 2–3 weeks — see a doctor regardless of your score:

    A cough that won't go away or worsens
    Coughing up blood, even a little
    Chest pain, worse with breathing or coughing
    Shortness of breath or wheezing
    Hoarseness that persists
    Unexplained weight loss or fatigue

    Understanding Your Lung Cancer Risk

    Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death worldwide, but it is one of the few cancers where screening a high-risk group with a simple, low-radiation CT scan has been proven to save lives. The challenge is that lung cancer is usually silent in its early, most treatable stages — which is exactly why identifying who should be screened matters so much. This calculator combines the 2021 USPSTF eligibility criteria with other established risk factors (family history, radon and occupational exposure, and COPD) to help you understand both your eligibility and your broader risk picture.

    What Your Risk Level Means

    Lower Risk
    Few major risk factors. If you have never smoked, routine CT screening is not recommended — but any persistent respiratory symptom still deserves attention.
    Moderate
    Some meaningful risk factors are present. If you have a significant smoking history, discuss whether screening is right for you with your doctor.
    High
    Multiple risk factors, likely including a substantial smoking history. If you meet the USPSTF criteria, annual low-dose CT screening is recommended.
    Very High
    A heavy smoking history plus additional risk factors. Screening and a conversation about quitting support should be prioritised as soon as possible.

    What to Do Next

    1. If you're eligible, ask your doctor for a low-dose CT scan. It takes seconds, uses about a fifth of the radiation of a standard chest CT, and is covered by most US insurers and Medicare for people who meet the criteria.
    2. If you still smoke, quitting is the single most powerful thing you can do. It lowers risk at any age and any pack-year history, and screening programmes include quit support for a reason — the two work best together.
    3. Test your home for radon. Radon is the leading cause of lung cancer in never-smokers. Home test kits are inexpensive, and mitigation is straightforward if levels are high.
    4. Don't ignore persistent symptoms. Screening is for people without symptoms. A cough, breathlessness, or chest pain lasting more than a couple of weeks warrants a prompt visit regardless of your score.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What is a pack-year?
    One pack-year equals smoking 20 cigarettes (one pack) a day for one year. It's calculated as (cigarettes per day ÷ 20) × years smoked. Smoking 10 a day for 40 years, or 40 a day for 10 years, both equal 20 pack-years.
    Is low-dose CT screening safe?
    Yes. LDCT uses far less radiation than a standard CT. The main downside is that it sometimes finds small nodules that turn out to be harmless but require follow-up. For high-risk people, the survival benefit of catching cancer early clearly outweighs these risks — which is why it carries a grade B recommendation.
    I quit smoking years ago — am I still at risk?
    Risk falls steadily after quitting but doesn't disappear. USPSTF screening applies to people who quit within the last 15 years and have a 20+ pack-year history. If you quit more than 15 years ago, routine screening is generally no longer recommended, but discuss your history with your doctor.
    Can never-smokers get lung cancer?
    Yes — about 10–20% of cases occur in never-smokers, driven by radon, secondhand smoke, air pollution, occupational exposures, and genetics. Never-smokers aren't eligible for routine screening but should always have persistent respiratory symptoms evaluated.
