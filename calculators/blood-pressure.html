What to do next:

    What do the two numbers mean?

    Blood pressure is written as two numbers — systolic over diastolic — measured in millimetres of mercury (mmHg).

    Top number
    Systolic
    The pressure in your arteries when your heart beats and pumps blood out.
    Bottom number
    Diastolic
    The pressure in your arteries when your heart rests between beats.

    About these guidelines

    This calculator uses the 2017 ACC/AHA Hypertension Guidelines, which lowered the threshold for Stage 1 hypertension from 140/90 to 130/80 mmHg — based on evidence that cardiovascular risk increases at these levels.

    A single reading is rarely enough to diagnose anything. Blood pressure naturally varies throughout the day, with stress, caffeine, exercise, and white-coat effect. If your reading falls in a concerning range, measure again on a different day before drawing conclusions — and always follow up with your doctor.

    Source: Whelton PK et al. 2017 ACC/AHA High Blood Pressure Guideline. Journal of the American College of Cardiology, 71(19), e127–e248.

    Reading Your Blood Pressure — What the Numbers Actually Tell You

    Blood pressure is expressed as two numbers, and understanding both is essential. The top number — systolic pressure — is the peak force your heart exerts with every beat. The bottom number — diastolic pressure — is the resting pressure between beats. Together they reveal how hard your cardiovascular system is working. The American Heart Association's 2017 guidelines, which this calculator uses, lowered the hypertension threshold from 140/90 to 130/80 after large clinical trials demonstrated that arterial damage begins well below the old cutoff. A single reading is a starting point; a pattern of readings is a diagnosis.

    What Your Score Means

    Normal
    Below 120/80 mmHg
    Optimal cardiovascular pressure. Continue the healthy habits that are protecting you now.
    Elevated
    120–129 / <80 mmHg
    Above optimal but not yet hypertension. Sodium reduction and increased aerobic activity are often sufficient to reverse this.
    Stage 1 Hypertension
    130–139 / 80–89 mmHg
    Confirmed with repeat measurements. Lifestyle modification is first-line; medication may be added if 10-year cardiovascular risk is elevated.
    Stage 2 Hypertension
    ≥140 / ≥90 mmHg
    Medication combined with lifestyle change is standard of care. Untreated hypertension at this level significantly increases stroke and heart attack risk.
    Hypertensive Crisis
    ≥180 / ≥120 mmHg
    Seek emergency care immediately. This level of pressure can cause acute organ damage, stroke, or aortic dissection within hours if untreated.

    What to Do Next

    1. Measure correctly and consistently. Sit quietly for 5 minutes before measuring. Keep your arm at heart level, don't cross your legs, don't talk. Take two readings one minute apart and average them.
    2. Track at least three readings before drawing conclusions. Blood pressure fluctuates with stress, caffeine, cold, and exertion. A pattern over days or weeks is what matters clinically, not a single number.
    3. Audit your sodium intake. The DASH diet trial demonstrated an 11 mmHg systolic reduction from dietary changes alone. Processed foods account for roughly 70% of sodium intake for most people.
    4. Bring your log to your doctor. A week of home readings is more clinically informative than a single office measurement and helps your provider make a much better-informed decision about treatment.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Which number matters more — systolic or diastolic?

    Both matter, but for adults over 50, elevated systolic pressure is the stronger predictor of cardiovascular events. Either number meeting hypertension criteria is sufficient to warrant clinical evaluation.

    Can blood pressure go too low?

    Yes. Symptomatic hypotension — typically systolic below 90 mmHg with dizziness or fainting — can cause falls and inadequate organ perfusion. It is especially important to monitor in older adults and those on antihypertensives.

    Does stress cause permanent high blood pressure?

    Acute stress causes temporary spikes. Chronic psychological stress is associated with sustained hypertension through activation of the sympathetic nervous system and cortisol pathways, compounded by stress-related behaviours like poor sleep and increased alcohol.

    How quickly can lifestyle changes lower blood pressure?

    Meaningful changes are measurable within 2–4 weeks of consistent intervention. Regular aerobic exercise produces an average reduction of 5–8 mmHg systolic. A DASH-aligned diet can produce similar reductions. Combined, these effects are comparable to a single antihypertensive medication.

    Are home blood pressure monitors accurate?

    Validated upper-arm cuff monitors certified by the American Medical Association are as accurate as clinical devices. Wrist devices are significantly less reliable. Always verify your cuff is the correct size for your arm circumference.
