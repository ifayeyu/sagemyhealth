Recommended next steps:

    Screening options — all reduce deaths

    Several tests are approved for average-risk screening. The best test is the one you'll actually complete.

    TestHow often
    ColonoscopyEvery 10 years (removes polyps in the same visit)
    FIT (stool test)Every year
    Stool-DNA (Cologuard)Every 1–3 years
    CT colonographyEvery 5 years

    A positive stool or CT result must be followed by a colonoscopy. People at higher risk are usually advised to have colonoscopy specifically.

    Symptoms to never ignore

    Screening is for people without symptoms. See a doctor promptly — at any age — if you have:

    Blood in the stool or rectal bleeding
    A lasting change in bowel habits
    Persistent abdominal cramps or pain
    Unexplained weight loss
    Feeling the bowel doesn't fully empty
    Unexplained iron-deficiency anemia

    Understanding Your Colorectal Cancer Risk

    Colorectal cancer is the third most common cancer worldwide, but it is also one of the most preventable — because it almost always develops slowly from precancerous polyps that screening can find and remove before they become dangerous. That's what makes timing so important: the goal isn't just to catch cancer early, but to stop it from ever forming. This calculator weighs the risk factors that matter most — your age, family history, personal history of polyps or inflammatory bowel disease, and lifestyle — to help you understand your risk level and when screening should begin.

    What Your Risk Level Means

    Lower Risk
    Few major risk factors. Follow the standard average-risk guidance: begin screening at 45 and keep up healthy habits that protect the bowel.
    Moderate
    Some meaningful risk factors. Screening on schedule matters, and lifestyle changes — more fibre, less processed meat, more movement — have real impact.
    High
    Multiple risk factors, possibly including family history. You may need to start screening earlier and use colonoscopy specifically. Discuss timing with your doctor.
    Very High
    Strong family history, prior polyps, or inflammatory bowel disease. You likely need earlier and more frequent colonoscopy surveillance guided by a specialist.

    What to Do Next

    1. If you're 45 or older and haven't been screened, book it. Average-risk screening now starts at 45. A colonoscopy every 10 years or a yearly stool test (FIT) both dramatically reduce colorectal cancer deaths.
    2. If you have a family history, ask about starting earlier. With a first-degree relative diagnosed under 60 — or two affected relatives — screening often begins at 40, or 10 years before the youngest case, with colonoscopy every 5 years.
    3. Improve the modifiable factors. More fibre, less red and processed meat, regular exercise, a healthy weight, limited alcohol, and not smoking all lower your baseline risk measurably.
    4. Never dismiss symptoms as "too young." Rectal bleeding, a lasting change in bowel habits, or unexplained anemia deserve prompt evaluation at any age — cases in younger adults are rising.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    At what age should I start screening?
    Average-risk adults should start at 45 (USPSTF and ACS). Those with a family history, prior polyps, or inflammatory bowel disease usually start earlier and screen more often — often from 40 or 10 years before a relative's diagnosis.
    Do I need a colonoscopy, or is a stool test enough?
    For average risk, both work: colonoscopy every 10 years, or a yearly FIT stool test (a positive result then needs a colonoscopy). Higher-risk people are generally advised to have colonoscopy specifically because it can both detect and remove polyps.
    Can lifestyle really lower my risk?
    Yes. Physical activity, a higher-fibre and lower-processed-meat diet, a healthy weight, limited alcohol, and not smoking are all linked to lower colorectal cancer risk. They don't replace screening but they meaningfully reduce your baseline.
    Why is bowel cancer rising in younger adults?
    Rates under 50 have climbed since the 1990s, prompting the screening age to drop to 45. The causes aren't fully understood but likely involve diet, obesity, the gut microbiome, and inactivity — which is why symptoms should never be brushed off as "too young."
