Insulin Sensitivity Index (HOMA-IR)

HOMA-IR (Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance) is the gold standard calculable measure of insulin sensitivity. Using your fasting glucose and fasting insulin lab values, it quantifies how effectively your cells respond to insulin — and how hard your pancreas is working to compensate.

Both glucose and insulin must be from a fasted blood draw.

⏱️ HOMA-IR requires fasting values — no food or caloric drink for at least 8–12 hours before the blood draw. Non-fasting values will produce inaccurate results.

    What HOMA-IR measures

    HOMA-IR (Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance) was developed at Oxford in 1985 and remains the most widely used non-euglycaemic clamp estimate of insulin resistance. It models the feedback loop between the pancreatic β-cells and insulin sensitivity in the liver. Higher scores mean your body needs more insulin to achieve the same glucose-lowering effect.

    Fasting insulin is the key value most people don't have — and the most informative. Fasting glucose alone can appear normal for years while insulin levels are already elevated. Tracking HOMA-IR over time (every 6–12 months) is a powerful way to measure the impact of lifestyle changes on metabolic health.

    Understanding Your Insulin Sensitivity Score

    Insulin sensitivity is the flip side of insulin resistance: it describes how effectively your cells respond to insulin's signal to take up glucose from the bloodstream. The Quantitative Insulin Sensitivity Check Index (QUICKI), calculated from your fasting glucose and fasting insulin levels, provides a validated, continuous measure of this efficiency. Higher values reflect better insulin sensitivity; lower values reflect increasing resistance. Unlike HOMA-IR, which is a direct measure of resistance, QUICKI is calculated in a way that provides better resolution in the high-sensitivity range, making it particularly useful for tracking improvements in people who are already fairly insulin sensitive. Both measures are derived from the same two laboratory values and are complementary.

    What Your Score Means

    Insulin Resistant
    QUICKI < 0.30
    Below this threshold indicates clinically significant insulin resistance. This range is associated with elevated risk for type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia, and cardiovascular disease.
    Borderline
    QUICKI 0.30–0.33
    Reduced insulin sensitivity. Lifestyle intervention can produce meaningful improvement, particularly increased physical activity and refined carbohydrate reduction.
    Normal Sensitivity
    QUICKI 0.33–0.38
    Adequate insulin sensitivity for metabolic health. Maintenance of current lifestyle and regular monitoring are appropriate.
    Good Sensitivity
    QUICKI > 0.38
    Healthy insulin sensitivity. Seen in metabolically fit individuals. The focus at this level is maintaining the behaviours that are producing this favourable result.

    What to Do Next

    1. Track your QUICKI alongside HOMA-IR for a complete picture. Both indices are derived from the same two values but capture slightly different aspects of the glucose-insulin relationship. Together they provide a fuller picture of your metabolic status and how it changes over time with intervention.
    2. Muscle is your primary insulin sensitivity organ. Skeletal muscle accounts for approximately 80% of insulin-stimulated glucose disposal. Building and maintaining muscle mass through resistance training is therefore one of the most direct interventions to improve insulin sensitivity — and its effects persist even when you're at rest.
    3. Time your carbohydrate intake around exercise. Post-exercise insulin sensitivity is elevated for 24–48 hours due to increased GLUT4 translocation in muscle cells. Consuming moderate-GI carbohydrates in the window immediately after exercise is associated with better glucose disposal and glycogen replenishment.
    4. Consider a glucose monitoring trial. A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) worn for 2–4 weeks provides detailed, real-time data on how your specific food choices, exercise, sleep, and stress affect blood glucose dynamics — information that laboratory values measured at a single time point cannot provide.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    What is the difference between QUICKI and HOMA-IR?

    Both are derived from fasting glucose and fasting insulin. HOMA-IR = (fasting glucose × fasting insulin) / 22.5. QUICKI = 1 / (log fasting insulin + log fasting glucose). HOMA-IR correlates better with metabolic disease at higher resistance values; QUICKI provides better resolution at the healthier end of the spectrum.

    How is fasting insulin different from fasting glucose?

    Fasting glucose measures the level of sugar in your blood. Fasting insulin measures how much insulin your pancreas is producing to manage that glucose. A high fasting insulin with a normal glucose is a classic early sign of insulin resistance — the pancreas is compensating by producing more insulin to achieve the same blood glucose result.

    Can stress cause insulin resistance?

    Yes. Cortisol (the primary stress hormone) is directly counter-regulatory to insulin — it raises blood glucose by stimulating hepatic glucose production and reducing cellular insulin sensitivity. Chronic psychological stress, through sustained cortisol elevation, is an independent contributor to insulin resistance.

    Is low-carb diet the only way to improve insulin sensitivity?

    No. While reducing refined carbohydrates and total glycaemic load is effective, the evidence also supports Mediterranean dietary patterns, higher dietary fibre, increased physical activity (especially resistance training), weight loss in those with excess visceral fat, sleep optimisation, and stress reduction as each independently improving insulin sensitivity.

    How long does it take to see improvement in QUICKI with lifestyle changes?

    Meaningful improvement in insulin sensitivity can be measured within 2–4 weeks of consistent lifestyle change (exercise + dietary modification). More substantial improvements typically appear at 8–12 weeks. Long-term sustained improvement requires sustained behaviour change — the benefits reverse if habits revert.
