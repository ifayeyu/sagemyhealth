    What is TDEE?

    Total Daily Energy Expenditure is the total number of calories your body burns each day — factoring in your resting metabolism, the energy used to digest food, and your physical activity. It's calculated using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, the most widely validated BMR formula in clinical use today.

    Understanding Your Total Daily Energy Expenditure

    Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) is the total number of calories your body burns in 24 hours — not just during exercise, but including every calorie used to breathe, think, maintain body temperature, digest food, and move through your day. It is composed of four components: Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR, the largest component at 60–70% of TDEE), the thermic effect of food (approximately 10%), non-exercise activity thermogenesis (NEAT — fidgeting, walking between rooms, standing), and exercise activity. The Mifflin-St Jeor equation used here is the most accurate published formula for estimating BMR without laboratory measurement, validated across multiple large studies in diverse populations.

    What Your Score Means

    Sedentary
    BMR × 1.2
    Little to no exercise. TDEE is primarily resting metabolism. Even small increases in daily movement produce meaningful caloric and metabolic improvements.
    Lightly Active
    BMR × 1.375
    Light exercise 1–3 days per week. Meets the minimum movement threshold but below the WHO physical activity recommendations for health.
    Moderately Active
    BMR × 1.55
    Moderate exercise 3–5 days per week. Meets WHO physical activity guidelines. The activity multiplier at this level meaningfully increases total energy needs.
    Very Active
    BMR × 1.725
    Hard training 6–7 days per week. Under-eating at this level is a common and performance-limiting mistake. Adequate fuelling is as important as the training itself.
    Extremely Active
    BMR × 1.9
    Elite athlete training loads. TDEE at this level can exceed 4,000–6,000 kcal/day in large, heavily training athletes. Underfuelling (Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport, RED-S) is a significant health risk.

    What to Do Next

    1. Use your TDEE as a starting point, then refine based on real-world data. Formulas carry error of ±10–15%. Track your food intake and weight for 2–3 weeks. If weight is stable at your estimated TDEE, the estimate is accurate. If you're losing or gaining unexpectedly, adjust the estimate up or down accordingly.
    2. Don't underestimate NEAT. Non-exercise activity thermogenesis can vary by 500–2,000 kcal/day between individuals with ostensibly similar activity levels. Desk workers vs those with physically active jobs, people who fidget vs those who sit still — these differences are enormous. Walking 8,000–10,000 steps per day contributes 300–600 kcal of daily expenditure.
    3. Understand that TDEE changes with body composition and weight loss. As you lose weight, your TDEE decreases — your body requires fewer calories to maintain a smaller body. Additionally, metabolic adaptation (the body's response to sustained caloric restriction) further reduces TDEE beyond what weight loss alone would predict. Recalculate every 5–10 kg of weight change.
    4. For weight management, create a deficit or surplus from your TDEE. A deficit of 500 kcal/day below TDEE produces approximately 0.5 kg (1 lb) of fat loss per week. A surplus of 250–500 kcal/day above TDEE with resistance training supports muscle growth. These are approximations — individual response varies.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Is the Mifflin-St Jeor equation accurate?

    It is the most validated formula available without laboratory measurement. It is more accurate than the Harris-Benedict equation (which tends to overestimate BMR) and is recommended by the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics for clinical use. However, all prediction equations have individual error of ±10–15%. Indirect calorimetry (metabolic cart measurement) is the gold standard but requires clinical equipment.

    Why does my calorie tracking not match my weight change?

    Multiple factors: food label regulations allow 20% error; cooking changes calorie availability of food; digestive efficiency varies between individuals (healthy gut microbiomes extract more calories from the same food); exercise calorie estimates from cardio machines and wearables overestimate by an average of 25–40%.

    Does eating less permanently slow my metabolism?

    Yes, to a degree — and this is one of the most important findings in obesity research. Caloric restriction reduces BMR through three mechanisms: loss of metabolically active tissue (muscle), reduced thyroid hormone activity, and reduced sympathetic nervous system activity. This explains why weight maintenance requires sustained effort after weight loss, not just a return to previous eating.

    What is NEAT and can I increase it?

    NEAT stands for Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis — all movement that isn't formal exercise (fidgeting, standing, walking between rooms, household tasks). Research by James Levine at Mayo Clinic showed NEAT can vary by up to 2,000 kcal/day between lean and obese individuals, largely explaining why some people seem to maintain weight effortlessly. Strategies to increase NEAT include a standing desk, walking meetings, taking stairs, and setting hourly movement reminders.

    Should I eat to my TDEE every day or average over the week?

    Your body's energy balance operates over days to weeks, not hour to hour. Eating slightly above TDEE some days and below on others — provided the weekly average meets your target — produces essentially the same body composition outcome as perfectly consistent daily intake. This gives useful flexibility for social eating and appetite variation.
