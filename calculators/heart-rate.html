How to use your zones:

    What are heart rate zones?

    Heart rate zones divide your effort level into five bands based on a percentage of your maximum heart rate. Training in different zones produces different physiological adaptations — Zone 2 builds aerobic endurance and fat metabolism, Zone 4 raises your lactate threshold, and Zone 5 develops peak power. Knowing your zones turns guesswork into deliberate training.

    Understanding Your Target Heart Rate Zones

    Heart rate is one of the most accessible and informative real-time indicators of exercise intensity. The concept of target heart rate zones — first formalised by Karvonen in 1957 — divides the spectrum from resting to maximum heart rate into training bands that correspond to different physiological adaptations. Each zone produces distinct effects: low zones build aerobic base and fat-burning capacity; high zones develop speed, VOↂ max, and cardiovascular power. Understanding which zone you're training in is the difference between training with a purpose and simply exercising. Maximum heart rate is estimated here using the Tanaka formula (208 − 0.7 × age), which is more accurate across the adult age range than the traditional 220 − age formula.

    What Your Score Means

    Zone 1 — Active Recovery
    50–60% HRmax
    Very light effort. Used for warm-up, cool-down, and active recovery days. Promotes blood flow without significant training load.
    Zone 2 — Aerobic Base
    60–70% HRmax
    Conversational pace. The zone that builds mitochondrial density, aerobic efficiency, and fat oxidation capacity. Elite endurance athletes do 70–80% of their training here.
    Zone 3 — Aerobic Power
    70–80% HRmax
    Moderate-hard effort. Improves lactate threshold. This is often the default zone for recreational exercisers — but can impair recovery if overused at the expense of Zone 2 and Zone 4.
    Zone 4 — Threshold
    80–90% HRmax
    Hard effort, can sustain for 20–60 minutes. Develops lactate threshold and race pace. Reserved for structured training intervals, not daily use.
    Zone 5 — VOↂ Max
    90–100% HRmax
    Maximum effort. Used in short, very hard intervals to develop peak cardiovascular capacity. Very high fatigue cost; requires substantial recovery.

    What to Do Next

    1. Build your aerobic base in Zone 2 before spending time in higher zones. The dominant model in elite endurance sport — polarised training — advocates spending 80% of training volume in Zone 2 and 20% in Zone 4/5. This produces better performance and recovery than spending most time in Zone 3.
    2. Measure your resting heart rate first thing in the morning. Resting heart rate is a powerful health metric: each 10 bpm decrease is associated with meaningful reduction in cardiovascular mortality risk. Elite endurance athletes often have resting rates of 35–50 bpm. A sudden increase of 5–10 bpm over baseline often signals overtraining or illness.
    3. Invest in an accurate heart rate monitor. Wrist-based optical HRM (as on most smartwatches) can lag by 5–15 seconds and is less accurate during high-intensity intervals. Chest strap monitors (Polar, Garmin HRM) are significantly more accurate and worthwhile for serious training.
    4. Use heart rate recovery as a fitness metric. Heart rate recovery — the drop in heart rate in the first minute after peak exercise — correlates strongly with cardiovascular fitness and mortality risk. A drop of less than 12 bpm in the first minute post-exercise is associated with higher cardiac event risk in clinical studies.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Is the 220 minus age formula accurate?

    The traditional formula (220 − age) has a standard deviation of roughly 10–12 bpm and significantly underestimates maximum heart rate in older adults. The Tanaka formula (208 − 0.7 × age) is more accurate based on a meta-analysis of 18,712 subjects. For most precise results, a maximal exercise test under clinical supervision gives individual maximum heart rate.

    What is Zone 2 training and why is everyone talking about it?

    Zone 2 has become prominent because research and elite coaching data show it is where the majority of mitochondrial adaptation occurs, with the lowest injury and recovery cost. It is typically the pace at which you can hold a full conversation. Most recreational exercisers chronically train too hard to be in true Zone 2, inadvertently spending most time in Zone 3.

    Can exercising at too high a heart rate be dangerous?

    For healthy individuals, high-intensity exercise carries low acute cardiac risk. For people with undetected coronary artery disease, cardiac arrhythmias, or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, high-intensity exercise can trigger events. The ACC/AHA recommends a medical evaluation before beginning vigorous exercise for men over 45, women over 55, and anyone with known or suspected cardiac risk factors.

    Does heart rate differ by sex?

    Yes, on average. Women typically have slightly higher resting heart rates and may have a somewhat lower maximum heart rate than men of the same age, though variation within each sex is larger than the average difference between sexes. Zone calculations are based on individual maximum heart rate, which largely accounts for this variation.

    What causes an elevated resting heart rate?

    Common causes include dehydration, stimulant intake (caffeine, certain medications), poor sleep, overtraining, emotional stress, infection, anaemia, hyperthyroidism, and cardiovascular disease. A resting rate consistently above 100 bpm (tachycardia) warrants medical evaluation.
