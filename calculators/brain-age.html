    What is brain age?

    Brain age is an estimate of how old your brain appears to be functioning — based on modifiable lifestyle factors rather than your birth year. Research in neuroscience shows that sleep, exercise, diet, social connection, and mental stimulation all directly affect neuroplasticity, cognitive reserve, and the rate of brain ageing.

    A brain age younger than your actual age means your lifestyle is preserving cognitive function. Older means certain habits may be accelerating decline — but these factors are reversible.

    What Your Brain Age Score Tells You

    The brain is not fixed hardware — it is a dynamic organ that responds to how you live, sleep, think, and move, throughout your entire life. Brain age estimates, derived from cognitive testing and lifestyle variables, have become an important research construct because they often diverge from chronological age in predictive ways. People whose brain age exceeds their calendar age show earlier onset of cognitive decline in longitudinal studies; those whose brain age is younger maintain sharper function later in life. This calculator synthesises the most predictive lifestyle and performance variables into an estimated brain age range — giving you a practical baseline for brain health optimisation.

    What to Do Next

    1. Prioritise aerobic exercise — the evidence is unusually strong. Regular aerobic exercise increases BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), stimulates hippocampal neurogenesis, and has been shown to increase grey matter volume in older adults. A 2011 PNAS study found a 2% increase in hippocampal volume after 12 months of aerobic training.
    2. Optimise sleep architecture, not just duration. Deep (slow-wave) sleep is when glymphatic clearance of toxic protein aggregates peaks. Alcohol and certain sedatives suppress slow-wave sleep even when total hours appear adequate. Consistent sleep timing is the most underappreciated sleep intervention.
    3. Control vascular risk factors aggressively. Hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and high LDL cholesterol are three of the most potent accelerators of brain aging. At midlife, these factors carry disproportionate long-term cognitive risk.
    4. Build cognitive reserve through genuine novelty. Learning an instrument, a new language, or an unfamiliar craft forces the brain to build new neural pathways. Difficulty is the mechanism; the brain does not benefit from easy repetition.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Is brain age the same as dementia risk?

    No. Brain age is a general estimate of neurocognitive aging, not a dementia screening tool. Conditions like Alzheimer's disease involve specific pathological processes that can only be detected with neuroimaging or cerebrospinal fluid analysis. This calculator does not screen for dementia.

    Can you improve brain age at any age?

    Yes. Neuroplasticity — the brain's capacity to reorganise and form new connections — persists throughout life. The rate slows with age, but meaningful improvement remains possible. Studies in adults over 70 have demonstrated measurable cognitive improvements from structured exercise, learning, and sleep interventions.

    Does diet affect brain aging?

    Significantly. The MIND diet — a hybrid of Mediterranean and DASH diets designed for neurological health — has the strongest evidence base, associated with a 7.5-year reduction in cognitive aging rate in observational studies. Key elements: leafy greens daily, berries twice weekly, olive oil, and fish twice weekly.

    How much does genetics affect brain aging?

    Twin studies suggest genetics explains 40–60% of cognitive aging variance at the population level. However, even carriers of high-risk genetic variants (including APOE ε4) show substantially different cognitive outcomes based on lifestyle.

    Are there medications that slow brain aging?

    No medications are currently approved for brain age reduction or dementia prevention in cognitively healthy adults. For cognitively healthy individuals, lifestyle interventions remain the most evidence-supported strategy.
