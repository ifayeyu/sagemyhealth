What this calculator doesn't account for
Paternal family history (father's side, aunts, grandmothers) — the Gail Model uses maternal first-degree relatives only. Paternal risk is clinically significant and should be discussed with your doctor.
Known BRCA1 or BRCA2 gene mutations — the Gail Model significantly underestimates risk in carriers. If you know you carry a BRCA variant, this result is not applicable to you.
Prior history of DCIS (ductal carcinoma in situ) or lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS) — these substantially alter risk and require specialist risk stratification.
Hormone replacement therapy (HRT), obesity, alcohol use, exercise — these modifiable factors affect risk and are covered by newer models such as Tyrer-Cuzick.
The Gail Model has known limitations in accuracy for non-white women; the NCI has published ethnicity-adjusted models. Your estimate has been adjusted, but please treat it as indicative rather than precise.

📅 Updated 2024–2025

What changed in breast cancer screening guidelines

Screening guidelines shifted significantly in 2024–2025, with major organisations recommending earlier and more personalised screening.

ACS 2024Annual mammograms starting at age 40 for all women of average risk. Women with above-average or high risk should begin earlier and may need supplemental MRI.
USPSTF 2024Updated final recommendation: biennial mammograms starting at age 40 for average-risk women (moved from 50). High-risk women should discuss earlier, more frequent screening.
FDA 2024Mandatory breast density disclosure on all mammography reports since September 10, 2024 across all US facilities — the first federal-level density notification standard.
ACS 2025Updated guidance on supplemental MRI for women with dense breasts who are at intermediate risk (15–20% lifetime) — previously only recommended for >20% lifetime risk.

Understanding Your Breast Cancer Risk Estimate

A breast cancer risk estimate is not a prediction that you will or will not develop cancer — it is a probability, derived from population-level data, that helps you and your clinical team decide what level of screening and preventive intervention is appropriate for your specific profile. The model used here draws on validated risk factors including age, family history, reproductive history, and prior biopsy findings — the same variables used in clinical risk models (Gail, Tyrer-Cuzick) that guide recommendations from major oncology organisations. Understanding your estimated risk allows you to have more specific, evidence-based conversations about mammography timing, supplemental MRI screening, lifestyle factors, and preventive medications.

What Your Score Means

Average Risk
5-year risk <1.7%
Below the threshold used to classify elevated risk. Standard annual or biennial mammography screening recommendations apply. Continue routine self-awareness and clinical breast exams.
Moderately Elevated
5-year risk 1.7–3%
Above average but below the high-risk threshold. Enhanced surveillance discussions with your provider are appropriate. Lifestyle modification has meaningful risk-reduction impact at this level.
High Risk
Lifetime risk >20%, or 5-year >3%
At this level, ACS and NCCN guidelines recommend discussing supplemental MRI screening, chemoprevention options (tamoxifen, raloxifene), and genetic counselling if not already completed.

What to Do Next

  1. Know the difference between estimated risk and a diagnosis. A high-risk estimate means more vigilant surveillance — it does not mean you will develop breast cancer. Most women with high estimated lifetime risk never do. The value of knowing is that it allows early detection and risk reduction.
  2. If your estimated risk is elevated, pursue genetic counselling. Variants in BRCA1, BRCA2, PALB2, CHEK2, and ATM genes significantly affect both risk magnitude and appropriate management. Knowing your genetic status allows clinicians to personalise recommendations in ways that population models cannot.
  3. Discuss supplemental screening with your radiologist or OB-GYN. Annual mammography alone misses some cancers — particularly in dense breast tissue. Supplemental ultrasound or breast MRI is recommended for higher-risk individuals. Ask about your breast density at your next mammogram.
  4. Focus on the modifiable risk factors within your control. Regular aerobic exercise (4–7 hours per week) is associated with a 20–40% risk reduction in observational studies. Limiting alcohol to under one drink per day reduces risk measurably. Maintaining a healthy weight after menopause is particularly important because adipose tissue becomes a primary oestrogen source.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a family history of breast cancer always mean high risk?

Not automatically. Risk depends heavily on the relationship (first-degree relatives carry more weight), the relative's age at diagnosis (earlier is higher risk), and how many relatives are affected. One aunt diagnosed at 70 carries very different risk implications than a mother and sister both diagnosed before 50.

What is the difference between a BRCA mutation and high calculated risk?

BRCA mutations are specific inherited genetic changes that dramatically increase risk — BRCA1 carriers have roughly 70% lifetime risk. High calculated risk from population models reflects statistical probability based on multiple risk factors. Genetic testing is the only way to determine mutation status.

Do birth control pills or hormone therapy affect risk?

Combined oral contraceptives are associated with a small increase in relative risk during use, which largely resolves within 10 years of stopping. Postmenopausal hormone therapy — particularly combined oestrogen-progestogen — carries a more established and longer-persisting risk increase.

Can mammograms cause breast cancer from radiation?

The radiation dose from a single mammogram is extremely low — roughly equivalent to 7 weeks of background radiation. The accepted benefit from early cancer detection far outweighs any theoretical radiation risk at standard screening frequencies.

Is dense breast tissue dangerous?

Dense breast tissue modestly increases cancer risk (roughly 1.5–2× average) and reduces mammogram sensitivity because dense tissue and tumours appear similarly on imaging. This is why women with dense breasts are often advised to have supplemental screening.
