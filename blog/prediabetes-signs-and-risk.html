Prediabetes: 1 in 3 Americans Have It and Don't Know.
These Are the Signs.

📅 July 2, 2026 ⏱ 7 min read 📋 SageMyHealth Editorial Team
Home › Health Guides › Prediabetes Signs & Risk

According to the CDC, 96 million American adults — approximately one in three — have prediabetes. Of those, an estimated 80% have no idea. There is usually no pain, no obvious symptom, no moment where the body signals that something has shifted. It can persist silently for years while the underlying metabolic disruption quietly progresses.

This matters now more than it has at any previous point in medical history. The rise of GLP-1 drugs, the growing understanding of insulin resistance as a root driver of multiple chronic diseases, and a generation of people increasingly motivated to manage their metabolic health before a diagnosis arrives — all of this makes prediabetes the most important health concept most people haven't fully understood yet. Here is what you actually need to know.

What Prediabetes Actually Is — and Why "Pre" Is Misleading

Prediabetes is a state of impaired glucose regulation: your blood glucose levels are higher than normal, but not yet high enough to meet the clinical threshold for type 2 diabetes. The term "pre" implies a buffer, a safe waiting room. It is more accurately described as the early stage of the same disease process — already causing low-grade vascular damage, already increasing cardiovascular risk, already indicating that your cells have begun to lose sensitivity to insulin's signal.

The underlying mechanism is insulin resistance. Your pancreas produces insulin normally, but your muscle, liver, and fat cells have become less responsive to it — requiring more insulin to move the same amount of glucose into cells. To compensate, the pancreas works harder. Over time, it begins to fail to keep up with demand. Blood glucose rises. First into the prediabetic range. Then, without intervention, into the diabetic range.

The reason prediabetes is not simply a milder version of diabetes is that the reversal window is real and significant. People with type 2 diabetes are managing a chronic condition. People with prediabetes are standing at a fork: the same path continues toward diabetes, or a different path — available through specific, achievable lifestyle changes — leads back toward normal glucose regulation.

Prediabetes is diagnosed by blood test, not symptoms. There are three tests clinicians use, each measuring glucose regulation from a different angle:

💡 One Test Is Often Enough to Start

A1C is the most commonly used screening test — it requires no fasting and gives a picture of average glucose over 2–3 months. However, A1C can be falsely low in people with iron deficiency anaemia or certain haemoglobin variants. If you have risk factors and your A1C looks normal but you're symptomatic, a fasting glucose or glucose tolerance test is worth discussing with your doctor.

Signs That Can Appear — and Why Most People Have None

Most people with prediabetes have no noticeable symptoms, which is the central public health challenge. However, as blood glucose levels rise through the higher end of the prediabetic range — and particularly as the condition approaches the type 2 threshold — some people experience subtle signals:

⚠️ Possible signals — most people with prediabetes will not experience these
  • Acanthosis nigricans — darkened, velvety patches of skin in skin folds (neck, armpits, groin); strongly associated with insulin resistance
  • Increased thirst and frequent urination — as glucose rises, the kidneys work harder to filter it, pulling more water with it
  • Energy slumps after meals — particularly the mid-afternoon crash following a carbohydrate-heavy meal; reflects dysregulated insulin response
  • Blurred vision — fluid shifts related to elevated glucose can temporarily affect lens shape
  • Slow wound healing — elevated glucose impairs immune function and blood vessel health
  • Tingling or numbness in hands and feet — an early sign of neuropathy; more common in higher-range prediabetes

"The absence of symptoms is not reassurance. It is the reason screening matters. Prediabetes is a diagnosis made by a blood test, not by how you feel."

The Reversal Window: What the Evidence Actually Shows

The most important and underreported fact about prediabetes is that it is reversible — and the reversal rates in clinical trials are striking. The landmark Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP), a large randomised controlled trial across 27 US centres, tested two interventions against a placebo control in people with prediabetes:

Diabetes Prevention Program — Key Results
58%
Reduction in progression to type 2 diabetes with lifestyle intervention (5–7% weight loss + 150 min/week exercise)
31%
Reduction with metformin alone — significantly less effective than lifestyle changes in this population
Source: Diabetes Prevention Program Research Group, NEJM 2002.

The specific lifestyle targets from the DPP: lose 5–7% of body weight (not 20%, not a dramatic transformation — 10–15 lbs for most people in the trial), and achieve 150 minutes of moderate physical activity per week. These are achievable targets, and the evidence for them is among the strongest in preventive medicine.

Dietary changes that show consistent evidence for improving insulin sensitivity: reducing refined carbohydrates and added sugars, increasing dietary fibre, prioritising protein and fat at meals to slow glucose absorption, and following a Mediterranean-style eating pattern. These are not restrictive diets — they are eating patterns with decades of supporting evidence.

On GLP-1 drugs: semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and similar GLP-1 receptor agonists are increasingly being used in people with prediabetes alongside significant obesity, given their effectiveness at reducing body weight and improving insulin sensitivity. This is currently an active area of clinical practice and evolving guidance — if you have risk factors and are interested in whether medication is appropriate for your situation, it is worth a specific conversation with your doctor.

The window is open. The question is whether you're in it.

Prediabetes is the condition where the trajectory is still changeable — where the cumulative metabolic work of lifestyle change has a 58% chance of preventing you from reaching a permanent diagnosis. That window does not stay open indefinitely. It narrows as beta cell function declines, as vascular damage accumulates, as insulin resistance deepens.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can prediabetes progress to type 2 diabetes without any warning?
Yes. Prediabetes is largely asymptomatic, and type 2 diabetes often develops gradually without dramatic symptoms. Without intervention, research suggests that 15–30% of people with prediabetes will develop type 2 diabetes within five years, and up to 70% may develop it over their lifetime. The absence of symptoms is not reassurance — it is the reason systematic screening matters.
What A1C level is prediabetes?
The American Diabetes Association defines prediabetes as an A1C between 5.7% and 6.4%. An A1C of 6.5% or above on two separate tests is diagnostic for type 2 diabetes. Below 5.7% is considered normal. A1C reflects your average blood glucose over the past 2–3 months. Note that it can be falsely low in people with iron deficiency anaemia or certain haemoglobin variants — if this applies to you, discuss alternative tests with your doctor.
Is prediabetes reversible?
Yes — and this is the most important fact most people don't know. The landmark Diabetes Prevention Program trial showed that a 5–7% reduction in body weight combined with 150 minutes of moderate exercise per week reduced progression to type 2 diabetes by 58% over three years. Many people who make sustained lifestyle changes return to entirely normal glucose regulation. The reversal window is real, significant, and available to most people with prediabetes who have not yet progressed.
Do GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic work for prediabetes?
GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, liraglutide) are FDA-approved primarily for type 2 diabetes and obesity, but are increasingly prescribed off-label for prediabetes with significant obesity given their effectiveness at weight reduction and insulin sensitivity improvement. The SURMOUNT and STEP trials showed significant metabolic improvements with semaglutide. Prescribing patterns are evolving rapidly. Speak with your doctor about whether this is appropriate for your specific situation and risk profile.
How often should I get screened if I have risk factors?
The American Diabetes Association recommends that adults with one or more risk factors be screened beginning at age 35, or earlier if risk factors are present. For people who test normal, rescreening every 3 years is standard if no new risk factors develop. Annual screening is appropriate for people already in the prediabetes range, given the active progression risk.
